Monte Carlo simulation for sizing and scenarios

A sizing multiplies several uncertain guesses, and neither the product of the likely values nor the product of the extremes says what will probably happen. Simulation draws the inputs many times and gives the distribution of the outcome, so you can state the chance of clearing the bar.

Why it exists

LeadIC

Try it first

Spend two or three minutes on this before reading anything else.

You are sizing a buyer protection programme. It costs ₹15 crore a year. Its value is eligible buyers × share who adopt × lift in their annual spend × annual spend. The team's ranges:

InputLowMost likelyHigh
Eligible buyers4 million5 million6 million
Adoption5%10%20%
Lift in spend among adopters3%6%12%
Annual spend₹6,000₹7,000₹8,000

The deck multiplies the most likely values: ₹21 crore. A sceptic multiplies the lows (₹3.6 crore) and the highs (₹115 crore) and says the range is useless.

What is the chance the programme pays for itself? Is ₹21 crore the most likely outcome?

Show answerHide answer

Simulate: draw each input from its range, say a triangular distribution with those three points, multiply, and repeat a million times.

  • Median ₹26.7 crore; 80% of outcomes fall between ₹15.6 and ₹44.2 crore; the mean is ₹28.6 crore.
  • Chance of exceeding ₹15 crore: about 92%.

The deck's ₹21 crore is not the most likely outcome, nor the median. Adoption and lift are skewed upwards (most likely 10%, but up to 20%), so their averages are above their most likely values, and the product of most likely values sits below the middle of the distribution. The sceptic's range needs every input at its extreme at once, which almost never happens: in a million draws the largest was ₹103 crore, and only about 3 in 10,000 exceeded ₹80 crore.

The decision-relevant statement: about a 9-in-10 chance of covering the cost, assuming the inputs are independent and the ranges are honest. The weakest link is the lift range, which an experiment could narrow.

Two analogies

Packing for a trip. You could pack for the worst weather on every day at once, snow and heatwave together, and carry three suitcases. Or you could think about the likely range of each day and pack for the combination that is plausible. Real days are rarely all extreme in the same way.

A commute with three legs. A walk, a train and a bus, each 10 to 30 minutes. Adding the worst cases says 90 minutes. Adding the typical ones says 50. Asking "how often will I be later than an hour?" needs the spread of the total, which you get by imagining many mornings.

IdeaPackingCommuteSizing
The inputsEach day's weatherEach leg's timeAdoption, lift, spend
Worst-case thinkingSnow and heat every dayAll three legs at their worstMultiply all the lows
Point thinkingPack for average weatherAdd the typical timesMultiply the most likely values
SimulationImagine many possible weeksImagine many morningsDraw inputs, multiply, repeat
The useful answerWhat fits most weeksChance of being later than an hourChance of clearing break-even

What do the two share? One sentence.

Compare with mineHide

When an outcome combines several uncertain parts, the useful answer is the distribution of the combination, which neither the all-worst nor the all-typical case describes.

Where each analogy breaks

  • Weather on different days is nearly independent. Business inputs often move together: a programme that more people adopt may help each of them less. Ignoring that changes the spread.
  • Commute times are measured every day. Sizing inputs are often guesses. A simulation of guesses is only as good as the guesses, though it makes them explicit.
  • Both have a natural unit of repetition. A sizing happens once; the probability is a statement about your uncertainty, not a frequency.

Self-test

Why?Why does multiplying the most likely values not give the most likely product?

Show answerHide answer

The most likely value of a product depends on the whole shape of each distribution. With skewed inputs, the product's centre sits away from the product of modes, here above it.

Concept

Monte Carlo simulation 1. write the outcome as a function of uncertain inputs value = buyers × adoption × lift × spend 2. give each input a distribution triangular(low, likely, high), lognormal, beta, uniform … 3. say how inputs move together (correlation, shared drivers) 4. draw all inputs, compute the outcome; repeat N times 5. report percentiles (P10, P50, P90), P(outcome > threshold), and which input drives the spread error of a simulated probability ≈ √(p(1 − p) / N) N = 10,000, p = 0.9 → ± 0.003
  • Inputs are beliefs, made explicit. A range of 3% to 12% for lift says what the team thinks, and the simulation shows what that belief implies.
  • Triangular or PERT distributions suit expert ranges with a most likely value. Use data where you have it: past experiment lifts, adoption of similar features.
  • Correlation matters. Positively related inputs widen the spread; negatively related ones narrow it. Model a shared driver directly when you can.
  • Tornado analysis shows which input's uncertainty matters most: vary one at a time across its range and see how much the outcome moves. That input is where to spend research.
  • Scenario planning is the same method applied to forecasts: simulate demand, capacity and prices together to see how often a plan breaks.

Self-test

Why?Why report P(value > cost) rather than the expected value alone?

Show answerHide answer

Two programmes with the same expected value can carry very different risk of losing money. The probability speaks to the decision directly, and leadership can weigh it against their appetite for risk.

How many simulation draws are enough?

Show answerHide answer

Enough that the numbers you report stop moving. For a probability near 0.9, 10,000 draws give about ±0.3 points; tail percentiles such as P99 need more.

Two of five draws are at or below ₹15 crore, and one is far above. With five draws you see the shape; with a million you get stable percentiles. Notice draw 5: high adoption and high lift together give a large value. If the two were negatively related, such draws would be rarer.

Half-worked: correlated inputs

Suppose broader adoption brings less committed buyers, so adoption and lift are negatively correlated (−0.6). Simulated: P10 ₹18.7 crore, median ₹26.5, P90 ₹37.3. With a positive correlation of +0.6: P10 ₹13.5, median ₹26.9, P90 ₹50.6. Find the chance of clearing ₹15 crore in each case and explain why the median barely moves.

Show answerHide answer

About 98% with negative correlation and 86% with positive, against 92% if independent. The median is about the same because correlation mostly changes how often the two inputs are extreme together, which moves the tails, not the centre. For a go or no-go decision near the threshold, the correlation assumption can matter more than the ranges.

Bare: which input to research

Holding the others at their medians, moving adoption from 5% to 20% moves the value from ₹12 to ₹48 crore. Moving lift from 3% to 12% does the same, ₹12 to ₹48 crore. Moving buyers across their range moves it from ₹22 to ₹32 crore, and spend from ₹23 to ₹31 crore. The research team can run one study. Which, and what kind?

Show answerHide answer

Adoption and lift dominate, equally in this tornado, each about four times the swing of buyers or spend. Lift is the better target, because an experiment on a pilot group measures it causally, and the pilot also gives an adoption rate. Buyers and spend are known well enough. The point is to narrow the inputs that drive the decision, not the easiest ones.

Predict, then play

Predict whether widening the top of the lift range from 12% to 20%, with everything else unchanged, raises the chance of clearing ₹15 crore by much.

Show answerHide answer

Only a little. It stretches the upper tail, which was already above break-even, and it raises the median somewhat. The chance of clearing the cost depends on the lower parts of the ranges. To lower the risk, evidence that rules out the low end is worth more than optimism about the high end.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
N = 1_000_000
tri = lambda lo, mode, hi: rng.triangular(lo, mode, hi, N)
value = tri(4e6, 5e6, 6e6) * tri(.05, .10, .20) * tri(.03, .06, .12) * tri(6000, 7000, 8000) / 1e7
np.percentile(value, [10, 50, 90])     # about 15.6, 26.7, 44.2 crore
(value > 15).mean()                     # about 0.92

Self-test

Why?A colleague uses uniform distributions instead of triangular for the same ranges. What happens to the spread, and why?

Show answerHide answer

It widens, because a uniform puts as much weight near the extremes as near the most likely value. The median moves towards the midpoint of each range.

Your P90 changes by ₹0.8 crore every time you rerun with 1,000 draws. What do you do?

Show answerHide answer

Use more draws, such as 100,000, until it is stable to the precision you report, and fix the random seed so a rerun reproduces the numbers.

Self-test

The simulation says 92% chance of clearing break-even. A year later the programme misses. Was the simulation wrong?

Show answerHide answer

Not necessarily: 8% events happen. Check whether the miss came from an input outside its range, which says the ranges were too narrow, or from a combination inside them. Track sizings against outcomes over many programmes to calibrate the team's ranges.

Self-test

For a board slide, would you show the Monte Carlo distribution or low, likely and high scenarios?

Show answerHide answer

Scenarios with the probability attached: "base case ₹27 crore; a 1-in-10 chance it is below ₹16 crore; a 1-in-10 chance above ₹44 crore". The distribution is the analysis; percentiles are how it is told.

Applications in pairs

Pair 1

Sizing a new category launch. Sellers, listings, conversion and price are each uncertain.

A warehouse capacity plan. Demand, pick rate and staffing are each uncertain.

Pair 2

A revenue forecast for next quarter. Finance wants a number and a range.

An experiment's projected annual value. The lift has a confidence interval.

What does each pair share?Hide

Pair 1 is sizing and capacity. Several uncertain inputs multiply or combine; the question is how often the outcome crosses a line: break-even, or capacity.

Pair 2 propagates measured uncertainty. Draw the lift from its interval, combine it with uncertain reach and persistence, and report the projection as a range rather than the point lift times traffic.

A well-known misuse

Precise-looking outputs from invented inputs. A simulation with ten thousand draws produces a crisp P90 of ₹44.2 crore, and the precision of the number is taken for the precision of the knowledge. The decimal reflects the arithmetic, not the inputs, which were three guesses each. Round, state the inputs on the same slide, and show what happens when the key range is wrong.

Self-test

A sizing deck shows 'P50 ₹26.71 crore'. What would you change?

Show answerHide answer

Round to ₹27 crore or even ₹25 to ₹30 crore, list the input ranges and their sources beside it, and show the effect of the most uncertain input. The second decimal implies knowledge nobody has.

In practice

  • Write the formula with finance or the business owner before any distribution.
  • Ground ranges in data: past launches' adoption, past experiments' lifts, shrunk towards typical values.
  • Ask two people for ranges, the owner and a sceptic, and run both.
  • Model correlation explicitly or through shared drivers. Test the result's sensitivity to it.
  • Report P10, P50, P90 and P(clears the bar), rounded, with the tornado.
  • Keep a log of sizings and outcomes. After a year, check whether 80% of outcomes fell in the P10 to P90 ranges.

Self-test

After a year, only 40% of outcomes fell inside the stated P10 to P90 ranges. What does that tell you?

Show answerHide answer

The ranges are overconfident: they should contain 80%. Widen future ranges, or anchor them on past outcomes. Typical overconfidence is a well-known pattern in expert estimates.

Nuances and contested ground

  • Is simulation false precision? Sceptics say three guesses per input do not deserve a probability distribution. Defenders say the guesses are being multiplied anyway, and simulation is the honest way to do it.
  • Triangular or something smoother? Triangles are easy to elicit and explain; PERT or lognormal shapes are smoother and often more realistic in the tails. The choice matters less than the ranges.
  • Should leadership see distributions? Some find percentiles clarifying; others anchor on the median and ignore the rest. Framing as "chance of clearing the bar" usually works best.
  • Who sets the ranges? Owners know the most and are the most optimistic. Separating who sizes from who benefits improves calibration but costs knowledge.

Self-test

Make the case for a simple low-likely-high table over a simulation.

Show answerHide answer

It is transparent, needs no tooling, and invites debate about each input. For a decision far from break-even, the simulation adds little. Simulation earns its keep near the threshold, or when many inputs combine.
