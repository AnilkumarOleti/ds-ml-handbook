C5 · Behavioural and growth analytics

Path and sequence analysis

Users do not move through a product in a straight line: they search, browse, go back and return. Sankeys show the main flows, sequence mining finds patterns that separate buyers from abandoners, and Markov chains turn journeys into a model that sizes the value of improving each step.

Try it first

Spend two or three minutes on this before reading anything else.

Sessions move between five pages until they buy or leave. From last month's click data, the chance of each next step:

FromSearchListingCartCheckoutBuyLeave
Home50%20%30%
Search20%55%25%
Listing25%10%20%45%
Cart15%60%25%
Checkout70%30%

Two teams pitch goals for next quarter. The listing team will raise listing → cart from 20% to 25%. The checkout team will raise cart → checkout from 60% to 70%. Both are taken from the "leave" share. Which goal is worth more to sessions that start on the home page, and why might a funnel chart mislead you?

Show answerHide answer

Treat the journey as a Markov chain and compute the chance of eventually buying from the home page: 6.5% today.

  • Listing → cart at 25%: conversion rises to 8.3%, +26%.
  • Cart → checkout at 70%: 7.6%, +17%.

The listing goal is worth more, though its change is smaller in points. Listings are visited again and again, after searches and after other listings, so a better listing page gets many chances per session; and every extra cart still passes the later steps. A funnel chart of "share reaching each step" (100% home, 54% listing, 15% cart, 9% checkout, 6.5% buy) shows where people drop, but not what a change at one step does once people loop back. 1

Two analogies

A board game with snakes and ladders. Your chance of finishing depends on every square and every arrow, including the ones that send you back. Fixing a square you land on often matters more than fixing a rare one near the end.

Traffic through a city. A count of cars at each junction tells you where traffic is. Where to widen a road needs the flows between junctions, including the cars that circle back.

IdeaBoard gameCity trafficUser journeys
StatesSquaresJunctionsPages or events
TransitionsDice and arrowsTurns at each junctionNext-step probabilities
LoopsSnakesCircling the blockSearch → listing → search
The questionChance of finishingWhere to widen a roadChance of buying; which step to fix
What a simple count missesHow often you revisit a squareWhere cars go nextRevisits and loops

What do the two share? One sentence.

Compare with mineHide

In both, the outcome depends on how the pieces connect, including the paths that loop back, so the value of improving one step comes from the whole network rather than from that step's own numbers.

Where each analogy breaks

  • Dice have no memory, and neither does a basic Markov chain. Real users do: someone on their third search is less likely to buy than someone on their first. Higher-order chains or features of the path fix part of this.
  • Traffic flows are physical. Journey probabilities are averages over very different users; splitting by segment often changes the picture.
  • Both are fixed boards. Changing one page can change how people use the others, so a chain's "what if" is a model estimate, not a test result.

Self-test

Why?Why can the share of sessions that ever reach the cart (15%) not be multiplied by the later rates to get conversion?

Show answerHide answer

Sessions can leave the cart and come back via a listing, so a session may pass the cart step several times. The funnel shares count sessions, not passes, and the chain accounts for the loops.

Concept

Sankey diagram width of each flow = number of sessions good for showing the main paths; the long tail is hidden sequence mining frequent ordered patterns: "search → filter → listing → cart" (PrefixSpan, SPADE); compare buyers with non-buyers Markov chain states, transition matrix P transient part Q, absorbing part R (buy, leave) fundamental matrix N = (I − Q)⁻¹ expected visits to each state absorption B = N · R chance of ending in buy or leave what if: change a transition, recompute B removal effect: send a state's traffic to "leave"; the fall in conversion is that state's importance
  • Sankeys are for showing, not for deciding. They make the top few paths vivid and lump everything else.
  • Sequence mining finds ordered patterns that distinguish groups, such as paths common among buyers and rare among abandoners. Patterns found are associations.
  • Markov chains turn many journeys into a small model you can compute with: the chance of buying from any page, the expected pages per session, and the effect of changing one step.
  • Removal effects are used for channel attribution: remove a channel from the chain and measure the loss. They describe the journey data, not the channel's causal effect. 2
  • Memory. A first-order chain assumes the next step depends only on the current page. Add the previous page, or the session count, as part of the state if behaviour depends on them.

Self-test

Why?What does N = (I − Q)⁻¹ give you, in plain words?

Show answerHide answer

For a session starting on each page, the expected number of times it visits every page before buying or leaving. Its row sums are the expected session length in pages.

Removing 'search' from the chain cuts conversion by 70%. Does that mean search causes 70% of purchases?

Show answerHide answer

No. It means 70% of purchase paths in the data passed through search. Without search, many users would find items another way. The removal effect is a property of observed paths, not a counterfactual.

Mechanics, faded

Worked: conversion from each page

Solving B = (I − Q)⁻¹R for the table in section 1 gives the chance of eventually buying from each page:

Starting pageHomeSearchListingCartCheckout
Chance of buying6.5%8.3%12.0%43.8%70.0%

A session on the home page visits 2.9 pages on average before it ends. Cart → checkout → buy is short and strong. The weakness is the listing page: only 12% of sessions that reach a listing end up buying, and listings are where sessions spend their visits.

Check one value by hand: from checkout, 70% buy directly. From cart: 60% go to checkout (then 70% buy), 15% go back to a listing (then 12.0% buy): 0.6 × 0.7 + 0.15 × 0.120 = 0.420 + 0.018 = 0.438.

Half-worked: three goals compared

Each goal moves one transition and takes the difference from "leave". From the home page, conversion becomes 8.3% with listing → cart at 25%; 7.6% with cart → checkout at 70%; 7.5% with checkout → buy at 80%. The checkout team says their change is the cheapest. What else do you need before ranking the goals?

Show answerHide answer

The chain gives the value of each change if it happens: +26%, +17%, +14%. To rank goals you also need how likely each team is to hit its target, what it costs, and whether the changes interact. Then check the model: a listing change that pushes marginal items into carts may also lower cart → checkout, which the chain holds fixed. Treat the chain as a sizing tool, and confirm with an experiment.

Bare: the long tail

The Sankey for the home page shows the top 10 paths, covering 38% of sessions. A PM concludes "most users go home → search → listing". What is wrong, and what would you show instead?

Show answerHide answer

62% of sessions follow paths outside the top 10, and the picture says nothing about them. Show the share covered on the chart itself, collapse paths into states and transitions (the Markov view covers everything), and add a sequence-mining comparison of buyers and non-buyers to find the patterns that matter.

Predict, then play

Predict whether raising checkout → buy from 70% to 90%, a big change, beats raising listing → cart from 20% to 25%.

Show answerHide answer

It does: checkout → buy at 90% gives 8.4%, slightly above the listing goal's 8.3%. A 20-point change at the last step is about worth a 5-point change at the listing, because only 9% of sessions reach checkout, while listings are visited by over half and revisited.

import numpy as np

# transient states: home, search, listing, cart, checkout; absorbing: buy, leave
Q = np.array([[0, .50, .20, 0, 0], [0, .20, .55, 0, 0], [0, .25, .10, .20, 0],
              [0, 0, .15, 0, .60], [0, 0, 0, 0, 0]])
R = np.array([[0, .30], [0, .25], [0, .45], [0, .25], [.70, .30]])
N = np.linalg.inv(np.eye(5) - Q)      # expected visits
B = N @ R                             # chance of buy / leave from each state
B[0, 0], N[0].sum()                   # 0.065 conversion, 2.9 pages per session

Self-test

Why?Why must every row of [Q R] sum to 1?

Show answerHide answer

Each row lists every possible next step from that page, including buying and leaving, so the probabilities must cover all outcomes exactly once.

Your chain is built from all sessions, but mobile users loop through search far more. What could go wrong?

Show answerHide answer

The pooled chain averages two different journeys and can misstate the value of a change for both. Build chains by segment, or add the segment to the state.

Self-test

Removing 'wish list' from the chain cuts conversion by 20%, yet a test that hid the wish list changed nothing. Explain.

Show answerHide answer

Motivated buyers use the wish list, so many purchase paths pass through it. Hidden, those buyers took another path. The removal effect measured where buyers walk, not what the wish list causes.

Self-test

Checkout is a fixed five-step form. Funnel or Markov chain?

Show answerHide answer

A funnel. With no loops and a fixed order, the funnel's step rates multiply to the conversion rate, and it is easier to read.

Applications in pairs

Pair 1

Which page to improve next. Browse, search, listing, cart.

Seller onboarding. Sign-up, verification, first listing, first sale, with sellers bouncing between steps.

Pair 2

Marketing channel attribution. Paid search, email, social, direct.

Support journeys. Help article, chat, call, resolution.

What does each pair share?Hide

Pair 1 uses the chain for what-ifs. Steps loop and repeat; the chain sizes the gain from improving each step before committing a team to it.

Pair 2 uses removal effects. They describe how much flow passes through each channel or support route. For marketing budgets, they must be checked against incrementality tests, because channels that sit on many paths may add little.

A well-known misuse

Markov attribution presented as causal. A removal-effect table credits email with 25% of conversions, and the email budget doubles. Removal effects redistribute credit along observed paths, like any attribution model. They cannot see that the people who open emails were going to buy anyway.

Self-test

A Markov attribution model gives email 25% of conversions. What would you need before increasing email spend?

Show answerHide answer

A holdout test: withhold email from a random group and measure the difference in conversion. That is the incremental effect, which can be far below the removal effect.

In practice

  • Define states with care: few enough to estimate well, specific enough to act on. Merge rare pages.
  • Check the memory assumption by comparing transitions by step number; add history to the state if needed.
  • Build chains by segment for decisions that target a segment.
  • Use the chain to size, then test. The what-if is a prioritisation tool.
  • Label Sankeys with coverage: "top 10 paths, 38% of sessions".
  • Validate events before modelling paths; one double-firing event distorts every transition.

Self-test

How would you validate a journey chain before using it to rank roadmap items?

Show answerHide answer

Simulate sessions from the chain and compare with real data: conversion, session length, share reaching each page. Then check its what-if predictions against past experiments that changed one step.

Nuances and contested ground

  • Markov models or machine learning? Sequence models such as RNNs or transformers predict journeys better but are harder to interrogate. Markov chains stay popular because their what-ifs are transparent.
  • Is removal-effect attribution better than last-click? It spreads credit more sensibly, but shares the same flaw: it describes paths, not causes.
  • Sessions or users? Session chains are simple and miss purchases that take several visits. User-level chains capture them and are harder to define.
  • How many states? More states capture more behaviour and need more data per transition. There is no general answer; validate against held-out data.

Self-test

Make the case for a simple funnel over a Markov chain in a leadership review.

Show answerHide answer

It is instantly readable, and for a mostly linear flow it gives nearly the same answer. The chain earns its place only when loops and revisits change which step is worth fixing.

Strong

Rung 3 · What ifIC

What is a removal effect, and why is it not a causal effect?

Follow-ups, weak and strong answersHide
  • Where is it used?
  • How would you get the causal number?

Weak

"It is how much conversion falls if you remove a channel."

Strong

The fall in modelled conversion when a state's transitions are redirected to "leave". It shows how much observed flow passes through that state. Users who took that path would often have found another way, so it overstates causal importance. The causal number needs a holdout test or another causal design.

Rung 4 · DesignLeadIC

Design a journey analytics capability for a marketplace with web and app.

Follow-ups, weak and strong answersHide

Weak

"A dashboard of Sankeys."

Strong
