A5 · Causal inference

Double/debiased machine learning

Flexible models can adjust for confounding that a linear regression misses, but a model tuned to predict does not give an honest causal coefficient. DML fits one model for the outcome and one for the treatment, then regresses residual on residual, with cross-fitting, so rough models still give a valid estimate and interval.

Why it exists

1
ICMLE

Try it first

Spend two or three minutes on this before reading anything else.

Sellers choose whether to offer free shipping. You want its effect on 90-day GMV, in ₹ thousand. You have 20,000 sellers and 20 covariates, including size, category, rating and tenure. Shipping choice and GMV both depend on those covariates in curved, interacting ways.

Three analysts report:

  • A: free-shipping sellers average 7.3 more.
  • B: a linear regression of GMV on free shipping and all 20 covariates gives 5.2.
  • C: a gradient-boosted model predicts GMV from free shipping and the covariates; switching free shipping on for everyone raises predictions by 2.3.

The data was simulated, so the true effect is known: 2.0. Why is each one off, and what would get it right?

Show answerHide answer

A ignores confounding: big, well-rated sellers both offer free shipping and sell more.

B adjusts, but only for straight-line effects. The confounding is curved, so most of it survives.

C models the curves, but a predictive model is tuned to predict GMV, not to isolate one coefficient. Regularisation shrinks and blurs the treatment's role, and nothing gives it a valid interval.

Double/debiased machine learning uses two flexible models: one predicts GMV from the covariates, one predicts free shipping from the covariates. It then regresses the part of GMV the covariates did not predict on the part of free shipping they did not predict. With cross-fitting, it gives 2.00, interval 1.84 to 2.16, here. It still assumes every confounder is among the 20 covariates. 1

Two analogies

Judging a runner's shoes. To see whether new shoes make a runner faster, first predict each runner's time from age, training and course. Separately, predict who buys the new shoes from the same things. Then ask: among the surprises, do runners who bought the shoes unexpectedly also run unexpectedly fast?

Grading a teacher. To credit a teacher fairly, predict each pupil's mark from their past marks and background, and predict which pupils were put in that teacher's class. A teacher who gets better-than-predicted marks from pupils they were not expected to get has shown something.

What do the two share? One sentence.

Compare with mineHide

Both remove what the background explains from the outcome and from the treatment, and judge the treatment only by whether its unexplained part lines up with the outcome's unexplained part.

Where each analogy breaks

  • Both assume the background is measured. A runner's motivation is not; neither is a seller's ambition. DML handles measured confounding flexibly and unmeasured confounding not at all.
  • Neither says how to fit the predictions. If the same data both fits a model and is scored by it, an overfitted model explains away too much. Cross-fitting predicts each unit from a model that never saw it.
  • Both imply one effect for everyone. The basic estimate is an average; heterogeneous effects need extensions such as causal forests or DR-learners.

Self-test

Why predict the treatment as well as the outcome? Is predicting the outcome not enough?

Show answerHide answer

Predicting only the outcome leaves the bias from any error in that model. Predicting both makes the remaining bias depend on the product of the two models' errors, which is much smaller when both are reasonable.

Concept

partially linear model Y = θ·D + g(X) + ε effect θ, any shape g D = m(X) + v treatment depends on X through m DML, partialling out 1. split the data into K folds 2. for each fold, fit on the other folds: ĝ(X) ≈ E[Y | X] m̂(X) ≈ E[D | X] and predict the held-out fold (cross-fitting) 3. residuals Ỹ = Y − ĝ(X) D̃ = D − m̂(X) 4. θ̂ = Σ D̃·Ỹ / Σ D̃² regress Ỹ on D̃ 5. standard error from the residuals; ordinary interval why it works: the error in θ̂ depends on (error in ĝ) × (error in m̂) so each may be fairly rough
  • Neyman orthogonality. The residual-on-residual estimate barely moves when either nuisance model is slightly wrong, because the first-order effect of each error cancels. Only the product of the two errors remains. 1
  • Cross-fitting. Each unit's residual comes from models fitted without it. This stops overfitting from making the residuals artificially small and keeps the interval valid.
  • Any learner can be used: gradient boosting, random forests, lasso, neural networks. Pick them by out-of-fold prediction error, as you would for any prediction task.
  • Binary treatments and effects that vary use the interactive version, which is the doubly robust AIPW score from matching and propensity scores with cross-fitting.
  • It does not fix the identification. No unmeasured confounders, overlap, and pre-treatment covariates are still needed.

Self-test

Why?Why is DML said to be 'debiased'? Debiased relative to what?

Show answerHide answer

Relative to plugging a regularised machine-learning model straight into the effect estimate. Regularisation and overfitting bias that plug-in; orthogonalisation and cross-fitting remove the first-order part of that bias.

Your treatment model predicts free shipping with an AUC of 0.99. What problem do you expect?

Show answerHide answer

Little variation in the treatment remains after removing what the covariates predict, so D̃ is tiny, the estimate rests on few units, and the interval is wide. It is the overlap problem in another form.

Sellers whose choice was well predicted, with D̃ near zero, contribute little: the estimate is driven by the unexpected choices.

Half-worked: the simulation, side by side

On the full simulated data, 20,000 sellers, true effect 2.0:

EstimatorEstimate
Raw difference7.25

Explain why the linear regression is still biased with all 20 covariates in it, and why the no-cross-fitting row landing near 2 is not reassuring.

Show answerHide answer

The covariates enter the truth through curves, squares, thresholds and an interaction. A linear term in each removes only the linear part of the confounding; the rest stays in the treatment coefficient. The no-cross-fitting row happens to land close here, but its residuals were computed on data the forests had memorised, so its standard error has no guarantee, and on other data the bias can be large. Cross-fitting is what makes the interval honest.

Bare: choosing the models

Your outcome model explains 60% of GMV's variance out of fold, and your treatment model has an AUC of 0.70. A colleague says the models are too weak for DML to work. Respond.

Show answerHide answer

DML needs the product of the two errors to be small, not each to be perfect, and weak models are not a reason for bias if they capture the confounding that exists. What the colleague should worry about instead: whether the covariates include the confounders at all, and whether a better model, tuned by out-of-fold error, explains more. Try a few learners, compare the residual errors, and check that the estimate is stable across them.

Predict, then play

A confounder adds 2.5 to the raw estimate. Each model captures 80% of the confounder's effect. Predict the remaining bias with only the outcome model, and with both.

Show answerHide answer

About 0.5 with the outcome model alone (20% of 2.5), and about 0.19 with both, roughly 20% of 20% of the original, slightly more because removing part of the treatment's variation also shrinks the denominator. That product is the whole idea.

from doubleml import DoubleMLData, DoubleMLPLR
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingRegressor, HistGradientBoostingClassifier

data = DoubleMLData(df, y_col="gmv_90d", d_cols="free_shipping", x_cols=covariates)
plr = DoubleMLPLR(data, ml_l=HistGradientBoostingRegressor(),   # E[Y | X]
                  ml_m=HistGradientBoostingClassifier(),        # E[D | X]
                  n_folds=5)
plr.fit()
plr.summary            # coefficient, standard error, interval

Self-test

Why?Why is the D̃ of a seller whose choice was perfectly predictable close to zero, and what does that mean for the estimate?

Show answerHide answer

If the covariates predict the choice, nothing is left over. Such sellers carry no information about the effect beyond what the covariates explain, so they get almost no weight. The estimate rests on sellers whose choice the covariates could not predict.

You get 2.0 with boosting and 3.1 with lasso for the nuisance models. What do you do?

Show answerHide answer

Compare their out-of-fold prediction errors. The better-predicting pair is more credible. A large gap suggests one learner misses the confounding's shape. Report the choice and the sensitivity, and consider an ensemble.

Self-test

Under the partially linear model, if the true effect varies across sellers, what does θ̂ estimate?

Show answerHide answer

A weighted average of the effects, with weights proportional to each seller's treatment variance given X. Sellers whose choice was most uncertain count most. That can differ from the average effect over all sellers.

Self-test

When would you choose plain AIPW over DML's partially linear model?

Show answerHide answer

For a binary treatment when you want the average effect over the whole population, rather than a variance-weighted average. With cross-fitted ML models, AIPW is itself a DML estimator.

Applications in pairs

Pair 1

Price and demand. The effect of a seller's price on units sold, with dozens of listing features.

Discount depth. The effect of a coupon's size, which marketing set by segment.

Pair 2

A feature adopted by choice. Effect of using saved searches on purchases.

An ops change. Effect of faster delivery promises, which depend on location and inventory.

What does each pair share?Hide

Pair 1 has continuous treatments set partly by rules on observed features, a natural fit for the partially linear model: residualise price and demand on the features. The price case is the classic one, an elasticity.

Pair 2 has binary treatments with rich, curved confounding, suited to the interactive version (AIPW with cross-fitting). The adoption case also has an unmeasured motivation problem that no model fixes.

A well-known misuse

Reading a causal effect off a predictive model. A churn model with high accuracy has "discount received" as a feature with a negative SHAP value, and someone concludes discounts reduce churn by that amount. The model learned to predict, with regularisation and correlated features sharing credit; the number is not an effect. DML is one way to answer the causal question with the same tools. 2

Self-test

A model's SHAP value for 'received coupon' is −0.04 on churn probability. Why is that not the effect of a coupon?

Show answerHide answer

Coupons were targeted, for example at at-risk users, and SHAP shares credit among correlated features to explain predictions. Neither makes it a causal effect. Estimate the effect with an experiment or a method that adjusts for the targeting.

In practice

  • Draw the causal diagram first and choose X from it. DML makes adjustment flexible; it does not decide what to adjust for.
  • Tune the nuisance models on out-of-fold error, separately, and report it.
  • Use five or more folds, and repeat the split a few times to see how much the estimate moves.
  • Check overlap through the treatment model's predictions, and trim if needed.
  • Cluster folds and standard errors when units share shocks.
  • Compare with simpler estimators. If DML and linear regression agree, the confounding was linear. If they disagree, explain why before reporting either.

Self-test

Repeating the cross-fitting with different random splits gives estimates from 1.7 to 2.4. What do you report?

Show answerHide answer

The median across splits, with a standard error that includes the split-to-split variation, as the DML literature recommends. Wide variation also suggests the nuisance models are unstable; check them.

Nuances and contested ground

  • Is flexibility worth the opacity? A linear model with chosen terms is easy to explain and audit. DML is a black box twice over. Proponents answer that its only output is one coefficient with an interval, and its assumptions are the same as regression's.
  • Does DML encourage throwing in every column? Easy flexible adjustment tempts analysts to include colliders and post-treatment variables. Covariate choice still needs causal reasoning.
  • How large must the data be? The guarantees are asymptotic. With a few thousand units and many covariates, the nuisance errors may not be small enough, and simulations calibrated to your data are the best check.
  • Which flavour? Partialling out, AIPW, targeted maximum likelihood (TMLE): they share the core idea and differ in finite-sample behaviour. Practitioners disagree on a default.

Self-test

Make the case for a well-specified linear regression over DML.

Show answerHide answer

When the confounders are few and known, a regression with sensible terms is transparent, efficient and auditable, and stakeholders can see what was adjusted. DML adds variance and complexity for little gain when the confounding is close to linear.

Strong

Fit one flexible model for the outcome and one for the treatment, both from the confounders, and estimate the effect by regressing the outcome's residual on the treatment's residual. Rung 3 · What ifICMLE

Why does DML need cross-fitting, and what happens without it?

Follow-ups, weak and strong answersHide
  • Is sample splitting wasteful?
  • How many folds?

Weak

"To avoid overfitting."

Strong

If a flexible model predicts the same data it was fitted on, its residuals are too small and correlated with the treatment's residuals in ways that bias the estimate and invalidate the standard error. Cross-fitting predicts each unit from a model that never saw it, and rotating the folds uses all the data. Five folds is common; repeat the split to check stability.
