A5 · Causal inference

Interrupted time series, CausalImpact and synthetic DiD

When a launch hits everyone at once, the only control is the past, and the past has a trend. This page covers forecasting the counterfactual from the pre-launch series, borrowing shared shocks from control series that the launch could not touch, and synthetic DiD for panels.

Why it exists

1
LeadIC

Try it first

Spend two or three minutes on this before reading anything else.

Free returns launched everywhere at once in week 27, so there is no control group. Weekly orders, in thousands, had grown by almost exactly 1,000 a week for six months, from 201 in week 1 to 226 in week 26, with week-to-week noise of about 3.

Weeks 27 to 34 averaged 238. The launch deck compares that with the eight weeks before, which averaged 222.5, and claims +7%.

One more fact: a neighbouring country, which did not get free returns, ran 2% above its own trend in weeks 27 to 34. A festival fell in week 31 in both.

What was the effect of free returns?

Show answerHide answer

Before and after credits the launch with six months of growth that was happening anyway. It is not an estimate of anything.

Interrupted time series. Extend the pre-launch trend: weeks 27 to 34 would have averaged 230.5. The gap is +7.5 thousand a week, +3.3%, with an interval of about 4.0 to 11.0 from the trend's own uncertainty.

With the neighbour as a control series. The festival lifted orders everywhere by about 2%, so the counterfactual is 230.5 × 1.02 = 235.1, and the effect is +2.9 thousand, +1.2%. The interval, roughly −0.2 to +6.0, now includes zero.

The honest readout: the trend-only method says +3%, but most of that coincides with a festival that lifted a comparable market by 2%. The best estimate is about +1%, and it is not clearly different from zero. If the decision needs more, measure the next region's launch with a staggered rollout.

Two analogies

A weight-loss diet started on 1 January. You lost 3 kg in a month. But you had been losing half a kilo a month all autumn, and everyone in your office loses a kilo in January after the holidays. The diet's share is what is left after both.

A new bus route. A shop's sales rose 10% the month the route opened. The shop was already growing 2% a month, and the shop next door, not on the route, rose 5% that month.

What do the two share? One sentence.

Compare with mineHide

A change measured against the past alone absorbs both the existing trend and anything else that happened at the same moment, so the counterfactual must extend the trend and borrow the shock from something unaffected.

Where each analogy breaks

  • Colleagues are people like you. A control series must move with the target before the launch, which is checked in data, not assumed from resemblance.
  • The diet has one start date. Launches are often announced early (anticipation) or rolled out over weeks (gradual onset), which blurs the break.
  • Both are short. The further a forecast runs past the launch, the wider its uncertainty and the more other changes creep in.

Self-test

Your control series is a category the launch could also affect, such as accessories bought with returned clothes. What goes wrong?

Show answerHide answer

The control is contaminated. If the launch raised accessory sales, the counterfactual is too high and the effect is understated. A control series must be one the intervention cannot touch.

Concept

interrupted time series (segmented regression) y_t = a + b·t + c·post_t + d·(t − t0)·post_t + e_t c = jump at launch, d = change in slope after it add seasonality terms; allow for autocorrelated errors CausalImpact (Bayesian structural time series) fit y_t on its own trend and season and on control series x_t using the pre-period only; forecast the post-period effect_t = observed_t − forecast_t, with a posterior interval cumulative effect = Σ effect_t synthetic difference-in-differences weights on control units, so their pre-trend matches the treated + weights on pre-periods, so they resemble the post-period + a unit fixed effect: levels may differ, trends must match
  • An interrupted time series uses the treated series' own past as the control. It is credible when the pre-period is long and stable and nothing else changed at the launch. 3
  • CausalImpact adds control series, such as other markets, categories or search interest, that are related to the outcome but not affected by the launch. It learns the relationship before the launch and uses it after. 1
  • Synthetic DiD sits between difference-in-differences and synthetic control. It weights control units like synthetic control, but lets the treated unit sit at a different level, as DiD does, and weights pre-periods towards the most relevant ones. 2
  • Uncertainty grows with the forecast horizon. The trend's slope is estimated, so its error is multiplied by the distance from the middle of the pre-period.

Self-test

Why?Why does the ITS interval widen the longer after the launch you measure?

Show answerHide answer

The counterfactual is the pre-period trend extended forward. Any error in the estimated slope grows with distance, and the chance that something else changes grows too.

When would you prefer synthetic DiD to plain synthetic control?

Show answerHide answer

When the treated unit is larger or smaller than any combination of controls can match in level, but its trend can be matched. Synthetic control needs the level matched too; synthetic DiD absorbs the level difference.

Mechanics, faded

Worked: the interrupted time series

From section 1: pre-period orders = 200 + 1.0 × week, residual sd 3, 26 weeks.

  • Counterfactual for weeks 27 to 34: 227, 228, … 234, average 230.5.
  • Effect: 238 − 230.5 = 7.5 thousand a week, 3.3%.
  • Uncertainty has two parts. The fitted line's error at the post-period's midpoint: 3 × √(1/26 + (30.5 − 13.5)²/1,462.5) = 1.46. The noise in eight observed weeks: 3 ÷ √8 = 1.06. Together √(1.46² + 1.06²) = 1.80, so the 95% interval is 4.0 to 11.0.

The line's error is the larger part, and it grows with the horizon. A single week's 95% band is ±6.3 at week 27 and ±8.4 at week 52.

This assumes independent weekly errors. Weekly sales are usually autocorrelated, which makes the true interval wider. Use errors that allow for it, or a model such as CausalImpact that includes it.

Half-worked: adding a control series

The neighbouring market tracked your market closely before week 27 (correlation 0.94 in weekly changes) and ran 2% above its own trend in weeks 27 to 34. A model that uses it gives a counterfactual of 230.5 × 1.02 = 235.1, with a standard error of about 1.6 for the average effect. Find the effect and its interval, and decide what to write.

Show answerHide answer

238 − 235.1 = +2.9 thousand, +1.2%, interval about −0.2 to +6.0. The point estimate is positive but zero cannot be ruled out. Write that the trend-only estimate of +3.3% is mostly explained by the festival, that the adjusted effect is about +1%, and that it is not yet distinguishable from zero. If the policy's cost needs more than +1% to break even, that is the decision-relevant line, not zero.

Bare: when did it start?

Free returns were announced on social media in week 25, two weeks before launch, and orders in weeks 25 and 26 ran 4 thousand above trend. What does this do to the estimate, and how do you handle it?

Show answerHide answer

Anticipation: the pre-period is contaminated, so the trend is fitted partly on treated weeks, and those weeks are counted as baseline. Fit the trend on weeks 1 to 24 only, treat the announcement as the start of the intervention, and report the effect from week 25. If people delayed purchases until free returns began, there may also be a dip before the launch and a bump after it, which inflates an effect read from the first few weeks.

Predict, then play

The 95% half-width for the eight-week average effect is 3.5 thousand with 26 pre-period weeks. Predict it with 8 weeks of pre-period, and with 52.

Show answerHide answer

About 7.8 with 8 weeks and 2.8 with 52. A short pre-period makes the slope uncertain, and that uncertainty is projected forward. A longer one helps, until the risk grows that the old trend no longer applies.

from causalimpact import CausalImpact   # one of several Python ports

# df: index = week, columns: y (your market), x1, x2 (control series)
ci = CausalImpact(df, pre_period=[1, 26], post_period=[27, 34])
print(ci.summary())        # average and cumulative effect, interval, posterior probability
ci.plot()                  # observed vs counterfactual, pointwise and cumulative effect

Self-test

Why?Why can the eight-week average be estimated more precisely than any single week's effect?

Show answerHide answer

The observation noise averages out over eight weeks. The trend's error does not; it is shared by all eight weeks, so it sets a floor on precision however many weeks you average.

A control series moved with yours before the launch but is driven by the same marketing calendar. Is that a problem?

Show answerHide answer

It is the point. A shared calendar is what lets it absorb the festival. The problem is only if the launch itself could move it.

Self-test

You run the same model with a fake launch date in week 15. It finds a 'significant' effect of +4%. What does that tell you?

Show answerHide answer

The model is finding effects that do not exist: its intervals are too narrow, or the pre-period has structural breaks. Fix the model, for example by allowing for autocorrelation or adding seasonality, until placebo dates give null results.

Self-test

A feature launched in one city, with fifty other cities untouched and two years of weekly data. Which row?

Show answerHide answer

Synthetic control or synthetic DiD, with placebo tests that apply the method to each untreated city in turn. An ITS on the one city would ignore fifty natural controls.

Applications in pairs

Pair 1

A nationwide app redesign. Everyone got it on the same day.

A new law on marketplace fees. It applied to every seller at once.

Pair 2

A TV campaign in one state. Other states saw none.

A warehouse opening near one city. Delivery times fell there only.

What does each pair share?Hide

Pair 1 has no untreated units. Interrupted time series with control series from outside the affected population, such as other countries, unaffected categories or search trends, is the main option.

Pair 2 has one treated unit among many. Synthetic control or synthetic DiD, using the untreated states or cities, gives a much stronger counterfactual than the unit's own trend.

A well-known misuse

The before-and-after chart in a launch announcement. A line rises, a vertical bar marks the launch, and the gap between the averages either side is presented as the effect. It credits the launch with the existing trend, the season and anything that happened that week. The fix is cheap: extend the trend, add a control series, and run the same analysis on a fake launch date.

Self-test

A leader shows a chart: weekly sellers onboarded rose 12% in the month after a new onboarding flow. What two questions do you ask?

Show answerHide answer

What was the trend before, and what else changed that month? Then: what does the same chart show for a comparable market without the new flow?

In practice

  • Use at least a year of pre-period for weekly data when you can, so seasonality is in the model.
  • Pick control series before looking at the post-period, and say why the launch cannot affect them.
  • Run placebo launch dates in the pre-period. A model that finds effects on fake dates has intervals you cannot trust.
  • Report cumulative and average effects with intervals, and the horizon you measured over. Keep the horizon short.
  • Plan a staggered rollout when a launch can be phased. Even two waves turn a fragile time-series estimate into a difference-in-differences.
  • Mark known events such as sales, festivals and outages on every chart.

Self-test

A PM asks for the effect of a launch 26 weeks after it happened. What do you warn about?

Show answerHide answer

Six months of extrapolation: the trend's error has grown, and many other changes have happened. Report the effect over the first few weeks with confidence, and anything later as increasingly a guess.

Nuances and contested ground

  • How many control series? More series fit the pre-period better but risk fitting noise and picking up contaminated ones. CausalImpact uses a prior that favours few; some analysts prefer a handful chosen by reasoning.
  • Bayesian or frequentist? CausalImpact reports a posterior probability of an effect, which is easy to misread as the probability that the launch worked. It depends on the model and priors like any other estimate.
  • Is ITS causal at all? Critics say a single series can never rule out coincident shocks. Defenders reply that with a long stable history, a sharp break exactly at the launch, and placebo checks, it is often the best evidence available.
  • Synthetic DiD or synthetic control? Synthetic DiD is more robust in many simulations, but it adds weights that are harder to explain. For a leadership audience, a transparent synthetic control with placebo plots may persuade more.

Self-test

Make the case for a simple ITS over CausalImpact for a leadership readout.

Show answerHide answer

It can be drawn and checked by eye: a straight line extended, a gap, a placebo date. Its assumptions are visible. CausalImpact's extra machinery helps with seasonality and controls, but adds choices a sceptical audience cannot see.

Weak

"Compare the average before with the average after."

Strong

Model the pre-launch trend and seasonality and forecast what would have happened, an interrupted time series, adding control series that the launch cannot affect, as CausalImpact does. Rung 3 · What ifLeadIC

Your ITS finds +5% at a fake launch date six months before the real one. What now?

Follow-ups, weak and strong answersHide
  • What are the likely causes?
  • When would you give up on the method?

Weak

"Fake dates do not matter; the real one is what counts."

Strong

The model is mis-specified: probably missing seasonality, a structural break, or autocorrelated errors making intervals too narrow. Add seasonality, allow for autocorrelation, try control series, and rerun placebos until they are null. If no specification passes, the series is too unstable for ITS, and the answer is a design: a staggered rollout or a holdout.
