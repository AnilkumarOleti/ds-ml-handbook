C6 · Opportunity sizing and research

Joining survey, PSAT and UXR findings to behavioural data

Surveys and interviews say why; behaviour says how many and how much. Joining them shows who answered and who did not, lets you weight answers back to everyone, tests stated reasons against what people do, and turns a research finding into a sized, testable opportunity.

Act 1

Why it exists

1
LeadIC

Try it first

Spend two or three minutes on this before reading anything else.

After the new checkout launched, a product satisfaction (PSAT) survey went to 100,000 recent buyers. 8% answered; average PSAT is 4.39 out of 5, and the PM's slide says "buyers love the new checkout". UX research interviewed 12 buyers; 6 were confused by how fees are shown.

Joining responses to behaviour by buyer ID shows who answered:

Buyer segmentShare of buyersResponse rateShare of respondentsMean PSAT
Heavy20%18%45%4.6
Medium30%9.3%35%4.4
Light50%3.2%20%3.9

What is a fairer estimate of PSAT among all buyers? What do the 12 interviews add, and how would you size the fee problem?

Show answerHide answer

Weight respondents back to the population. Heavy buyers answered at more than five times the rate of light buyers, and they like the product more. Weighted by segment: 0.2 × 4.6 + 0.3 × 4.4 + 0.5 × 3.9 = 4.19, and the share satisfied falls from 81% to 75%. The weights cost precision: 8,000 answers carry the information of about 5,000.

Weighting fixes only what was measured: within each segment, people who answer may still be happier than those who do not. 1

The interviews say what is wrong and why, which no score can. Six of twelve does not mean half of buyers; it means the problem is real and worth sizing. Size it with behaviour (drop-off at the fee step, fee-info taps, support contacts about fees) and a short in-product question to a random sample at that step. Then test a clearer fee display.

Two analogies

A restaurant's comment cards. The cards come mostly from regulars who love the place and the occasional furious diner. The silent majority who found it fine and never came back leave no card. The owner who reads only cards misjudges both how good the food is and why tables are empty.

A doctor's appointment. The patient's account of symptoms says what hurts and when; the blood test says how much and whether it is changing. Neither alone gives the diagnosis.

What people sayCardsThe patient's accountSurveys, PSAT, interviews

What words giveReasonsWhere it hurtsWhy, and what to change
What numbers giveHow many come backHow much, how fastHow many, how much it costs

What do the two share? One sentence.

Compare with mineHide

What people say explains why but comes from those who chose to speak, and what they do shows how much and for whom, so each corrects the other.

Where each analogy breaks

  • Comment cards cannot be weighted. Linked surveys can: when responses join to behaviour, you know who did not answer.
  • A patient is one person. Twelve interviews are twelve people chosen by a recruiter, which shows what exists, not how common it is.
  • Doctors order the right test. Analytics often has only the data that happened to be logged; qual research may reveal what should have been.

Self-test

Why?Why can twelve interviews establish that a problem exists but not how common it is?

Show answerHide answer

Twelve people chosen for availability or fit are not a random sample, and twelve is too few to estimate a share. One clear case shows the problem is possible; behaviour or a random survey sizes it.

Concept

join what people say to what they do (by user ID, consented) survey responses + behaviour before and after the survey non-response compare respondents with everyone on behaviour you have weight respondents to the population (post-stratification, raking) cost: effective sample size n_eff = (Σw)² / Σw² still assumes respondents resemble non-respondents within cells stated vs revealed check what people say against what they do ("price is why I left" vs. response to a price change) sequence qual finds what and why → quant sizes how many and how much → experiment tests whether changing it helps → qual explains surprises PSAT, CSAT, NPS: link to retention and spend to learn what a point is worth
  • Linking is the key move. A survey joined to behaviour reveals who answered, lets you weight, and lets you ask whether satisfaction predicts what people later do.
  • Sampling decides validity more than sample size: triggered surveys after support contacts reach unhappy users; surveys in a newsletter reach engaged ones. Random sampling from the population of interest is the default.
  • Weighting corrects for measured differences and shrinks the effective sample. 2 It cannot correct for differences you did not measure.
  • Stated reasons are hypotheses about behaviour. People explain their choices with reasons that sound sensible, not always the ones that drove them.
  • Qual and quant are a loop: interviews suggest what to measure, data sizes it, experiments test it, and interviews explain surprising results.

Self-test

Why?Why does weighting reduce the effective sample size?

Show answerHide answer

A few respondents with large weights count for many people, so their individual noise counts many times. The estimate behaves as if it came from fewer, equally weighted respondents.

A satisfaction survey is triggered only after a support chat. What will it measure?

Show answerHide answer

Satisfaction among buyers who had a problem and contacted support, a very different group from buyers overall. Useful for support quality; misleading as a product-wide score.

Act 2

How it works

4
LeadIC

Mechanics, faded

Worked: weighting PSAT

  • Response rates: heavy 18%, medium 9.3%, light 3.2%; overall 8%. Respondent shares: 45%, 35%, 20%.
  • Unweighted mean: 0.45 × 4.6 + 0.35 × 4.4 + 0.20 × 3.9 = 4.39.
  • Weighted to the population: 0.2 × 4.6 + 0.3 × 4.4 + 0.5 × 3.9 = 4.19.
  • Weights per respondent: heavy 0.44, medium 0.86, light 2.5. Effective sample: 5,010 of 7,990, 63%.

A 0.2-point drop sounds small, but light buyers are half the base and the least satisfied. The weighted share satisfied is 75%, not 81%, and the gap between segments is the more useful finding.

Half-worked: stated against revealed

Asked why they stopped buying, 40% of lapsed buyers chose "prices too high". A price-cut test for lapsed buyers raised their return rate by 1 point; a delivery-speed test raised it by 3 points, although only 15% had cited delivery. How do you reconcile them, and what would you tell the pricing team?

Show answerHide answer

Stated reasons reflect what is easy and acceptable to say; price is the default answer. Behaviour in randomised tests shows what changes decisions. Tell the pricing team the survey is a signal that price perception matters, but the measured lever for win-back is delivery speed. Follow up with interviews of lapsed buyers who did return after the delivery test, to understand why.

Bare: sizing an interview finding

Six of 12 interviewees were confused by fees at checkout. Design the measurement that tells you how many buyers are affected and what it costs.

Show answerHide answer

From behaviour: drop-off at the fee step against other steps; taps on fee-info icons; back-navigation from the fee line; support contacts mentioning fees. From a short in-product question shown to a random 2% of buyers at the fee step: "Is anything unclear about the total?" Then size lost orders from the drop-off gap, and test a clearer fee display with an A/B test to confirm the cost and the fix.

Predict, then play

Predict what happens to the weighted estimate and the effective sample size if light buyers' response rate falls from 3.2% to 1%.

Show answerHide answer

The weighted estimate barely moves, since it is anchored to population shares, but light respondents' weights grow large and the effective sample falls sharply, so the estimate gets noisier. Worse, the few light buyers who still answer are likely to be unusual ones, which no weight can fix. Low response in a key group is a reason to change how you ask, not only how you weight.

import pandas as pd

r = survey.merge(users[["user_id", "segment"]], on="user_id")          # consented join
pop = users.segment.value_counts(normalize=True)
resp = r.segment.value_counts(normalize=True)
r["w"] = r.segment.map(pop / resp)                                     # post-stratification
weighted_psat = (r.psat * r.w).sum() / r.w.sum()
n_eff = r.w.sum() ** 2 / (r.w ** 2).sum()
# raking (several margins at once): weights adjusted in turn to each margin until they all match

Self-test

Why?Why weight by behavioural segment rather than by demographics?

Show answerHide answer

Behaviour is what you have for everyone, and it is often what drives both response and satisfaction. Demographics may be unknown for many users and less related to the answers.

After weighting by segment, respondents in the light segment still bought twice as often as non-respondents in it. What does that tell you?

Show answerHide answer

Non-response within the segment is still related to engagement. Add finer cells or more margins, such as purchase frequency within segment, and treat the estimate as an upper bound on satisfaction.

Self-test

Response to the PSAT survey doubled after a reminder email. Is the new estimate more trustworthy?

Show answerHide answer

More respondents help if the reminder reached less engaged buyers. Check whether the second-wave respondents look more like the population. If they are the same kind of people, precision rose but bias did not fall.

Applications in pairs

Pair 1

NPS by seller tier for a quarterly business review.

CSAT after support chats for the support team's goals.

Pair 2

Interviews before redesigning search filters.

A diary study of how sellers manage stock.

What does each pair share?Hide

Pair 1 are scores that need weighting and linking. Who answers drives the number; linking to behaviour shows who is missing and whether the score predicts retention or spend.

Pair 2 are qualitative discovery. They find problems and language for them; their output is hypotheses and designs, sized and tested afterwards with data.

A well-known misuse

Quoting survey scores as if they described all users. A product review shows an average PSAT from respondents, who are the most engaged fifth of the user base, and declares success, while light users, half the base, are quietly unhappy. Joining responses to behaviour exposes it in an afternoon.

Self-test

A team reports NPS rose from 32 to 40 after a launch. What two questions do you ask?

Show answerHide answer

Did the mix of respondents change, for example more heavy users answering, and does the rise show up in behaviour, such as retention or referrals? Weight to a fixed population and check against behaviour before crediting the launch.

In practice

  • Sample at random from the population the decision is about, and record who was asked.
  • Link responses to behaviour with consent; compare respondents with non-respondents on every metric you have.
  • Weight to the population and report the effective sample size; show segment results, not only the total.
  • Calibrate scores against later behaviour: what is a point of PSAT worth in retention?
  • Run the loop: interviews to find, data to size, tests to fix, interviews to explain.
  • Quote people in reports. A sentence from a buyer often moves a team more than a chart.

Self-test

How would you make PSAT useful for prioritisation rather than a vanity score?

Show answerHide answer

Link it to retention and spend to estimate what a point is worth; break it down by segment and journey step; pair low-scoring steps with interview findings; and track it on a weighted, fixed population so changes reflect the product, not the respondents.

Prove you know it

9
LeadIC

Nuances and contested ground

  • Is NPS worth tracking? Critics say one question with arbitrary cut-offs predicts little. Supporters say its simplicity makes it a shared language. Linking it to behaviour settles its value for your business.
  • Weight or not? Weighting removes known bias and adds variance. With extreme weights, some teams prefer reporting by segment unweighted.
  • Who owns the insight? Research and analytics teams often work separately. Joint ownership of questions, rather than handing over findings, makes the loop work.
  • Privacy. Joining what people say to what they do is powerful and sensitive. Consent, aggregation and access controls decide what is acceptable.

Self-test

Make the case for running interviews before looking at any data.

Show answerHide answer

Data can only answer questions about what was logged, framed by what the team already believes. Interviews surface problems and words nobody thought to measure, so the analysis that follows asks better questions.

Model answer

unweighted 4.39 because heavy buyers are 45% of respondents. Weighted: 0.2 × 4.6 + 0.3 × 4.4 + 0.5 × 3.9 = 4.19. Weights of 0.44, 0.86 and 2.5 cut the effective sample from about 8,000 to 5,000. Within segments, happier buyers may still answer more, so 4.19 may still be optimistic.

Rung 3 · What ifLeadIC

40% of churned buyers say price drove them away, but a price-cut test barely moved win-back. Explain.

Follow-ups, weak and strong answersHide
  • Which do you trust?
  • What would you do next?

Weak

"The test was too small."

Strong

Stated reasons are what people find easy to say; price is the default. A randomised test shows what changes behaviour. Trust the test for the lever, treat the survey as a signal about price perception, and interview buyers who responded to other tests, such as faster delivery, to learn what actually brings them back.

Rung 4 · DesignLeadIC

Design a voice-of-customer programme that product teams can act on.

Follow-ups, weak and strong answersHide
  • How do research and analytics work together?
  • How do you avoid survey fatigue?

Weak

"A monthly NPS survey."

Strong

Random, rotating samples so no buyer is asked often; responses linked to behaviour and weighted; scores calibrated against retention and spend; open-text tagged and validated; quarterly interview rounds on the weakest journey steps; findings sized with behavioural data and tested with experiments; reports that pair numbers with quotes; joint research and analytics ownership of the question list.

Rung 5 · JudgmentLead

A PM's launch review says 'buyers love it' based on a survey of respondents. You know light buyers are underrepresented and less happy. What do you do?

Follow-ups, weak and strong answersHide
  • How do you say it in the review?
  • What do you offer instead?

Weak

Says nothing, since the survey was positive.

Strong

Before the review, shows the PM the respondent mix and the weighted result, so the correction is theirs. In the review: heavy buyers love it; light buyers, half our base, are lukewarm, and interviews point to fee confusion. Offers a sizing and a test of the fee display as the next step, which turns a correction into a plan.
