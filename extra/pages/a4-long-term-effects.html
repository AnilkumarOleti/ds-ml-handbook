Novelty effects and long-term holdouts

A two-week test measures the first two weeks. Users react to change itself, then settle, or learn and gain more later. This page covers reading the lift over time, using new users as a control for novelty, and the long-term holdout that measures what launches add together.

Why it exists

1
LeadIC

Try it first

Spend two or three minutes on this before reading anything else.

A new "picked for you" carousel on the home page ran for two weeks against the old page. The lift in items viewed per user, by day:

Day 1: +8.0%. Day 4: +4.6%. Day 7: +3.0%. Day 10: +2.2%. Day 14: +1.8%.

The readout says +3.5%, the average over the fourteen days, and the PM's annual plan books +3.5%. Among users who signed up during the test, and so had never seen the old page, the lift was about +1.5% on every day.

What is the carousel worth in the long run? What would you write in the readout?

Show answerHide answer

The daily lift is falling and flattening out, and new users, for whom nothing is new, sit at about 1.5% from the start. Both point to a novelty effect that decays towards a lasting lift of roughly +1.5%.

The fourteen-day average of 3.5% is 2.4 times the lasting effect. Week one averaged 5.0%, week two 2.1%. Even the last week overstates it, because the decay has not finished.

The readout should say: the carousel is a win, worth about 1.5% at steady state, with the evidence (the daily curve and the new-user cut). Book 1.5%, and confirm it with a holdout that keeps a small share of users on the old page for a quarter.

Two analogies

A new café on the high street. In the first week it has a queue round the corner. Everyone wants to try it. By the third month the queue has gone and a steady crowd of regulars is left. Judge it by opening week and you will open three more.

A new gym membership in January. Attendance is high for three weeks, then most members settle into twice a month. The gym's revenue depends on that later number, which no January count shows.

IdeaCaféGymProduct change
What you see firstOpening queueJanuary attendanceThe first week's lift
Why it is inflatedCuriosityResolutionsUsers explore anything new
What lastsRegularsThe habit that stuckThe steady-state lift
Who shows the lasting level earlyPeople who moved to town this monthMembers who joined in JuneNew users who never saw the old version
How to measure itCount customers in month threeCount visits in MarchA long run, a decay fit, or a holdout

What do the two share? One sentence.

Compare with mineHide

In both, the first response mixes curiosity with value, and only time, or people for whom it is not new, separates the part that lasts.

Where each analogy breaks

  • Both only decay. Product changes can also grow: users who first resist a new layout learn it and use it more. That is a primacy or learning effect, and a short test understates it.
  • The café sees everyone. An experiment sees only the users who arrived during the test, so heavy users dominate its first days.
  • Nothing else changes in the café story. In a product, other launches, seasons and competitors move the long-run number too, which is why a holdout, not a longer test, is the clean measure.

Self-test

A test's lift rises from +0.5% in week one to +2% in week three. What is the likely story?

Show answerHide answer

A primacy or learning effect. Users had to learn the change before it helped them. The test was too short to see the full effect, and the steady state may be higher still.

Concept

observed lift(t) = lasting lift + novelty(t) novelty(t) → 0 as users get used to the change often close to (first lift − lasting) × e^(−t/τ) signs of novelty daily lift trends down new users show the lasting level from day 1 heavy users, who saw it most, decay fastest signs of learning daily lift trends up; returning users gain most long-term holdout a small share of users kept on the old product for months, across many launches measures what all the launches did together surrogate a short-term metric that predicts the long-term one well enough to decide on
  • The lift is a function of time, not one number. Plot it by day, or by days since each user's first exposure, before reading any average.
  • New users are a natural control for novelty. They never saw the old version, so nothing is new to them. Their lift is a rough estimate of the lasting one, though new users differ from established users in other ways too.
  • A long-term holdout answers a different question from any single test: what did this quarter's launches add, together, after novelty, interactions and cannibalisation. 1
  • Surrogates make long-term effects decidable in a short test, if a short-term metric (or a combination of several, a surrogate index) has been shown to predict the long-term outcome. 2
  • User learning can change behaviour beyond the feature itself. Google found that showing fewer, better ads raised ad clicks in the long run because users learned to trust ads more, an effect only a months-long holdout revealed. 3

Self-test

Why?Why do new users give a cleaner view of the lasting effect than returning users?

Show answerHide answer

Novelty is a reaction to change. New users have no old version to compare against, so their response contains no novelty. It may still differ from the lasting effect for established users, because new users behave differently in general.

A team ran twelve tests this year with lifts summing to 14%. The year-long holdout shows 5%. Name three reasons.

Show answerHide answer

Novelty: tests read early, inflated lifts. The winner's curse: significant tests overstate their effects. Interactions and cannibalisation: launches compete for the same users and attention, so lifts do not add.

How it works

Mechanics, faded

Worked: reading a decaying lift

The daily lifts in section 1 follow 1.5% + 6.5% × e^(−(day − 1)/4) closely. The pieces:

  • Lasting lift, the level the curve flattens to: 1.5%. New users confirm it.
  • Novelty at day 1: 8.0% − 1.5% = 6.5 points, decaying with a time constant of four days, a half-life of 2.8 days.
  • Fourteen-day average: 3.54%. Week one 4.97%, week two 2.10%. By day 14 the curve is at 1.75%, still 0.25 points above the lasting level.

Two practical readings follow. Report the week-two or last-days lift alongside the average, and treat the new-user lift as a floor-to-central estimate. And extend the test, or keep a holdout, when the daily curve has not flattened by the end.

Half-worked: sizing a long-term holdout

You have 8,000,000 active users. Orders per user per quarter average 0.9, standard deviation 2.4. Leadership asks for a holdout that keeps some users off every launch for a quarter. Two-sided 5%, 80% power. The 1% holdout: 80,000 users, standard error 2.4 × √(1/80,000 + 1/7,920,000) = 0.0085, so the smallest detectable change is 2.8 × 0.0085 = 0.024 orders, 2.6%. Finish the 2% and 5% holdouts, and say what each costs if the quarter's launches really are worth 5%.

Show answerHide answer

2%: 160,000 users, detects 1.9%, and forgoes 160,000 × 0.9 × 5% = 7,200 orders a quarter. 5%: 400,000 users, detects 1.2%, at a cost of 18,000 orders. A quarter's worth of launches usually aims at several percent, so 1 to 2% is enough to see it. A 5% holdout buys precision you only need if launches are expected to add very little. The cost falls on a fixed group of users, so rotate them each quarter.

Bare: the lifts that do not add up

Twelve launches this year had lifts of 2.1, 1.4, 0.9, 1.8, 0.6, 1.2, 0.8, 1.5, 1.1, 0.7, 1.0 and 0.9%. The finance deck adds them to 14% (compounded, 14.9%). The year-long 2% holdout shows +5.0%, interval 3.1% to 6.9%. Which number goes in the plan for next year's launches, and what changes in how tests are read?

Show answerHide answer

The holdout's 5%: it is the only number that measures the launches together, in the long run. The gap of about 9 points is the sum of novelty, the winner's curse and overlapping launches. Plan with a discount of roughly two-thirds on summed test lifts, read each test's lift from its last week or from new users, and shrink test estimates towards a prior (see partial pooling and shrinkage).

Predict, then play

The carousel's novelty has a half-life of 2.8 days. Predict how long the test must run before its overall average is within 1.5 times the lasting lift, and within 1.25 times.

Show answerHide answer

About six weeks (40 days) for 1.5 times, and about eleven weeks for 1.25 times, even though the novelty has a half-life under three days. The average falls slowly because the early days stay in it. Running longer is an expensive way to remove novelty. It is cheaper to read the later days or the new users, or to fit the decay.

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import curve_fit

day = np.arange(1, 15)
lift = np.array([8.0, 6.56, 5.44, 4.57, 3.89, 3.36, 2.95, 2.63, 2.38, 2.19, 2.03, 1.92, 1.82, 1.75])
f = lambda t, L, A, tau: L + A * np.exp(-(t - 1) / tau)
(L, A, tau), _ = curve_fit(f, day, lift, p0=[1, 5, 3])
L, tau            # about 1.5 points lasting, time constant 4 days

Self-test

Why?Why is the fourteen-day average a poor estimate even though it uses all the data?

Show answerHide answer

It estimates the average effect during the test, which includes the novelty. The decision is about the effect after the test, when the novelty is gone. More data on the wrong quantity does not help.

The decay fit gives a lasting lift of 1.5% with a wide interval, 0.2% to 2.8%. What do you do?

Show answerHide answer

Ship if the lower end is acceptable, and keep a small holdout to measure the steady state directly. A fitted curve extrapolates, so its interval is wider than a direct measurement's.

Self-test

A notification test shows a lift that does not decay at all over four weeks, but unsubscribes are slowly rising in the treatment arm. What long-term risk is hidden?

Show answerHide answer

Notification fatigue. Users who unsubscribe can no longer be reached, so the channel's future value shrinks. The short-term lift borrows from it. Treat unsubscribes as a guardrail and measure reachable users at the end of a holdout.

Applications in pairs

Pair 1

A redesigned search results page. Week one +5%, then falling.

A "new" badge on listings. Clicks on badged items jump, then fade.

Pair 2

A keyboard shortcut for power sellers. Use is low at first, then climbs.

Fewer but better ads. Ad revenue falls at first, then recovers as users trust ads more.

What does each pair share?Hide

Pair 1 is novelty. Attention goes to whatever changed. The first-week lift is curiosity and fades.

Pair 2 is learning. The value appears only after users adjust, so a short test understates it, and the second case can look like a loss. Both call for a longer view: an exposure-day chart and a holdout.

A well-known misuse

Summing test lifts into a forecast. A growth team presents a year of wins adding to 20% while the business grew 4%. Every number was measured honestly, in a two-week window, on the tests that passed. The sum counts novelty, chance and overlap as growth. Teams that keep a holdout typically find the combined effect is a fraction of the summed lifts.

Self-test

Your team's tests summed to 20% last year and the company grew 4%. A VP asks whether the experimentation programme works. What do you say?

Show answerHide answer

The tests may well have worked, but summed short-term lifts are the wrong measure. Propose a standing holdout to measure the programme's combined effect, and shrink future estimates. The answer to the VP should be a number from the holdout, not a defence of the sum.

In practice

  • Chart the lift by day as a standard part of every readout, with new and returning users separately.
  • Run at least two full weeks, and longer for visible UI changes, which have the strongest novelty.
  • Keep a standing holdout of 1 to 2% from all launches, refreshed each quarter or half, and report the combined effect against the summed lifts.
  • Use the holdout's ratio to discount forecasts. If summed lifts overstate the holdout by three times, plan with a third.
  • Validate surrogates on history before using them to decide long-term questions, and re-check them each year.
  • Hold back the riskiest changes longer: notifications, pricing, ad load, where short-term gains can cost long-term reach or trust.

Self-test

Product asks to end the standing holdout because "users in it are getting a worse product". How do you respond?

Show answerHide answer

They are, slightly, and that is the cost of knowing whether launches work. Keep it small, rotate users through it so nobody stays for long, and exempt critical fixes such as security and bugs. Without it, planning rests on numbers known to be inflated.

Nuances and contested ground

  • How long is long-term? A quarter catches novelty and most learning. Effects on churn, trust or seller supply can take a year. Longer holdouts cost more and drift more.
  • Is a universal holdout fair to the users in it? Some teams find it unacceptable to withhold improvements. Others argue the held-out users get exactly what everyone had a quarter ago, and that rotation spreads the cost.
  • Can a model replace the holdout? Surrogate indices and decay fits are cheaper and faster, but rest on assumptions a holdout does not need. Most teams use models for decisions and holdouts to check the models.
  • Should the readout use the decayed or the measured effect? Reporting a fitted lasting effect invites the charge of second-guessing the data. Reporting the test average invites booking novelty as growth. Reporting both, with the reasoning, is the usual compromise.

Self-test

Make the case against a universal holdout.

Show answerHide answer

It is small, so it detects only large combined effects. It drifts away from the active user base. It complicates every launch, and it costs revenue every day. Validated surrogates and careful readouts may give most of the answer for far less.

Rung 1 · Recall the idea

What is a novelty effect, and how would you detect one?

Follow-ups, weak and strong answersHide
  • What is the opposite effect called?
  • Why do new users help?

Weak

"Users like new things, so run the test longer."

Strong

Users react to change itself, so an early lift fades as the change becomes familiar. Detect it with the lift by day or by days since first exposure, which trends down, and with new users, who never saw the old version and show the lasting level from the start. The opposite, a primacy or learning effect, trends up.

Rung 2 · Why

A two-week test of a home page carousel averaged +3.5%, falling from +8% on day 1 to +1.8% on day 14. New users show +1.5% throughout. What do you report?

Follow-ups, weak and strong answersHide
  • The PM says the test was positive on every day. Why not book 3.5%?
  • How would you confirm the lasting effect?

Model answer

the lift decays towards about 1.5%, which the new users confirm. The test average of 3.5% is 2.4 times that. Report a lasting lift of about 1.5%, with the daily curve and the new-user cut as evidence, and confirm it with a holdout kept on the old page for a quarter.

Weak

Reports the 3.5% average, or calls the test unreliable and asks to rerun it.

Strong

Must have. Separates the decaying novelty from the lasting level and uses the new users.

Strong signal. Proposes a holdout or decay fit, and points out that the last day still overstates the steady state.

Rung 3 · What ifLeadIC

Twelve launches had lifts summing to 14%. The year-long holdout shows 5%. Explain the gap.

Follow-ups, weak and strong answersHide
  • Which of your reasons is largest, and how would you find out?
  • What do you change in how tests are read?

Weak

"The holdout is too small to trust."

Strong

Rung 4 · DesignLeadIC

Design how your company measures the long-term effect of product launches.

Follow-ups, weak and strong answersHide
  • How big is the holdout, and how often is it refreshed?
  • Which launches get a longer holdout of their own?

Weak

"Run every test for three months."

Strong

Standard day-by-day and new-user charts in every readout; a standing universal holdout of 1 to 2%, sized for the combined effect a quarter of launches should produce, refreshed each quarter; dedicated longer holdouts for changes with long-term risk such as notifications, pricing and ad load; surrogate metrics validated on past tests; forecasts discounted by the holdout's ratio to summed lifts.

A launch that the CEO championed shows a decaying lift that is heading towards zero. The team wants to report the two-week average. What do you do?

Weak

Reports the average because it is technically correct, or calls the launch a failure in public.
