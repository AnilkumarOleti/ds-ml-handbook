Power and false positive rates by simulation

Power formulas and standard tests assume tidy data. Skewed revenue, clustered sessions and unusual designs break those assumptions without any warning. Simulating many A/A and A/B tests on data like yours counts how often your actual analysis is wrong.

Why it exists

1
ICMLE

Try it first

Spend two or three minutes on this before reading anything else.

A test randomises users, 10,000 per arm, and the metric is revenue per session. Users average 5.3 sessions, a quarter of them buy anything, and revenue per user is very skewed: mean ₹755, standard deviation ₹3,300.

The dashboard runs a t-test on all sessions. Before trusting it, you simulate 2,000 A/A tests, with no real difference, from data built to look like yours.

The dashboard's test calls 17.5% of them significant at the 5% level. A user-level test calls 5.6%. And for a real +15% lift, the standard power formula promises 68% power, while simulation of the user-level test gives 56%.

What is going on in each case, and what would you change?

Show answerHide answer

The 17.5%: sessions from the same user are correlated, since heavy buyers buy in many sessions, but the session-level t-test treats them as independent. Its standard error is too small, so it finds "effects" three and a half times as often as it should. The fix is to analyse at the randomisation unit: revenue per user, or revenue per session with the delta method, both near 5% in simulation. See randomisation unit and ratio metrics.

The power gap: the formula assumes the average is close to normal. With revenue this skewed, 10,000 users are not enough for that to hold well, and a few huge spenders dominate the variance. Simulation measures the test you will actually run. Capping revenue at the 99th percentile raises simulated power to 67%, at the cost of measuring a slightly different metric.

Neither problem shows up in a formula. Both show up the moment you simulate. 1

Two analogies

A fire drill. You do not learn whether an evacuation plan works by reading it. You stage a fire that does not exist and see how many people get out in time. Then you stage one in a different place.

A flight simulator. Pilots practise engine failures in a machine that knows the right answer. The simulator does not tell you how often engines fail; it tells you how the pilot performs when one does.

IdeaFire drillFlight simulatorSimulated tests
What is stagedA fire that is not thereA failure that did not happenAn effect you set yourself
What is knownWhere the fire isWhat failedThe true effect, including zero
What is measuredHow many got outWhether the pilot recoveredHow often the test is significant
With nothing happeningFalse alarmsUnnecessary abortsFalse positive rate (A/A)
With something happeningEvacuation rateSuccessful recoveriesPower

What do the two share? One sentence.

Compare with mineHide

Both test a procedure by running it many times in a world where the right answer is known, so its error rates can be counted rather than assumed.

Where each analogy breaks

  • A drill uses the real building. A simulation uses a model of your data, which can be wrong. Resampling real data, or real A/A splits, keeps it honest.
  • A simulator pilot knows it is a test. A statistical test cannot tell, which is the point: its behaviour on fake data is its behaviour on real data, if the fake data is realistic.
  • Drills are expensive. Simulated tests are cheap, so run thousands; the result has its own error, about √(p(1 − p)/N).

Self-test

Why?Why run A/A simulations on resampled real users rather than on data drawn from a normal distribution?

Show answerHide answer

The problems you are looking for, such as skew, within-user correlation and outliers, are properties of the real data. A normal model would hide them and report that everything is fine.

Concept

A/A simulation (false positive rate) repeat many times: split real users at random into two arms, or simulate from a model fitted to real data, with no effect run exactly the analysis you will use false positive rate = share of p-values < 0.05 (should be 5%) and the p-values should look uniform power by simulation same, but add a known effect to one arm power = share significant vary n, effect, metric definition, analysis Monte Carlo error of a rate p from N simulations ≈ √(p(1 − p) / N) N = 2,000, p = 0.05 → ± 0.5 points
  • Simulate the analysis you will run, end to end: the same filters, metric definitions, capping, CUPED and test. A formula covers only the idealised version.
  • Use for non-standard designs: cluster randomisation, switchbacks, ratio metrics, quantile metrics, sequential looks, several metrics with corrections. Closed forms are often missing or wrong.
  • How to add an effect matters: multiply revenue by 1.05 for everyone, or add it to buyers only, or change conversion. Each gives different power. Choose the one that matches what the feature would do.
  • Uniform p-values under A/A are the full check: a histogram that piles up near zero means too many false positives, one that piles up near one means a test that is too conservative.
  • Real A/A tests on the platform catch problems simulation cannot, such as bucketing bugs. Run both. 2

Self-test

Why?Your A/A simulation gives a false positive rate of 5.7% from 2,000 runs. Is the test miscalibrated?

Show answerHide answer

Probably not. The simulation error is about ±0.5 points, so 5.7% is within about 1.5 standard errors of 5%. Run more simulations if the difference would matter.

Why can simulated power differ from the formula's for a skewed metric?

Show answerHide answer

The formula assumes the difference in means is normal with a known variance. With heavy tails, the sample variance is unstable and the test statistic is skewed at this sample size, so the real test can reject more or less often than the formula expects. Here it rejected less often; in other data it can be the other way, which is why you simulate.

Mechanics, faded

Worked: reading the A/A results

AnalysisFalse positive rate, 2,000 A/A runs
t-test on revenue per session, sessions as units17.5%
t-test on revenue per user5.6%
Revenue per session, delta method on user totals5.7%
t-test on revenue per user capped at the 99th percentile4.8%

The simulation error at 5% is ±0.5 points, so the last three are all consistent with 5%. The first is three and a half times too high. Sessions per user average 5.3 and within-user correlation is substantial, so the session-level standard error is badly understated. Every "significant" session-level result on this dashboard is suspect.

Half-worked: power by simulation

The team wants 80% power for a +10% lift. Roughly how many users per arm does the capped metric need, and what would you check before adopting the cap?

Show answerHide answer

Power of 38% at 10,000 means the effect is about 1.65 standard errors (1.96 − 0.31). Reaching 2.8 standard errors needs (2.8 ÷ 1.65)² ≈ 2.9 times the users, about 29,000 per arm, and a simulation at that size to confirm it. Before adopting the cap: that the A/A false positive rate is still 5% (it is, 4.8%), that the cap is computed from pooled data or pre-period data, not separately per arm, and that the business accepts that the capped metric understates gains concentrated among the biggest spenders.

Bare: a design with no formula

A delivery-fee test will switch fees on and off by city and hour (a switchback), across 12 cities for 4 weeks, with demand that has strong daily cycles and carries over between hours. How do you know what effect it can detect?

Show answerHide answer

Simulate it. Take last year's hourly orders for the 12 cities, apply the planned switching schedule, add a known effect in the treated hours, with and without carry-over into the following hour, and run the planned analysis thousands of times. Measure power and, with no effect added, the false positive rate. Try schedule variations, such as two-hour blocks or buffers between switches, and pick the one with valid error rates and the most power. See interference and marketplace experiments.

Predict, then play

Predict what happens to the gap between the session-level and user-level false positive rates as the number of sessions per user grows.

Show answerHide answer

It widens. The variance inflation from clustering grows roughly like 1 + (sessions per user − 1) × the within-user correlation, so more sessions per user means a larger understatement. With one session each, the two tests agree.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def simulate(users, lift, n_sims=2000, cap=None):
    hits = 0
    for _ in range(n_sims):
        a = rng.choice(users, size=10_000)                 # resample real users
        b = rng.choice(users, size=10_000) * (1 + lift)
        if cap is not None:
            c = np.percentile(np.concatenate([a, b]), cap)
            a, b = np.minimum(a, c), np.minimum(b, c)
        hits += stats.ttest_ind(a, b, equal_var=False).pvalue < 0.05
    return hits / n_sims       # lift = 0 gives the false positive rate

Self-test

Why?Why compute the cap from both arms pooled, not separately for each arm?

Show answerHide answer

A cap computed per arm depends on that arm's own data, including the effect, which can bias the comparison. Pooled data or a pre-period cap treats both arms the same.

A/A simulation of a new metric gives p-values that pile up near 1. What does it mean?

Show answerHide answer

The test is too conservative: its standard error is too large. It will rarely give false positives but also has less power than it should. Look for a variance formula that overcounts, such as a correction applied twice.

Self-test

Resampling real users gives a false positive rate of 5% for a metric. Six months later, a festival test shows strange results. Why might the earlier check not have covered it?

Show answerHide answer

The resampled period had no festival. Variance and skew during a festival can be very different. Simulate on festival periods too, or on the most volatile weeks you have.

What does each pair share?Hide

Pair 1 has clustering. The analysis unit is smaller than the randomisation unit, so naive tests understate variance. A/A simulation exposes it; the fix is to analyse at the randomisation unit or use the delta method or cluster-robust errors.

Pair 2 has awkward distributions. Extreme values or a quantile metric make normal-theory power unreliable. Simulation gives the true power, and shows whether capping or a different metric helps.

A well-known misuse

Running an A/A test once and declaring the platform sound because it was not significant. One A/A test has a 95% chance of passing even with a broken method. The check is the rate over many splits, real or simulated, and the shape of the p-value histogram. Large platforms run continuous A/A tests for this reason. 2

Self-test

A single A/A test came back with p = 0.4. The platform owner says it is validated. What do you say?

Show answerHide answer

One test cannot measure a rate. A test with a 17% false positive rate passes a single A/A test 83% of the time. Run hundreds of A/A splits on historical data and look at the rate and the histogram.

In practice

  • A/A-simulate every new metric before it goes on a dashboard: 1,000 or more resampled splits, a rate and a histogram.
  • Use real data: resample users or historical periods rather than inventing distributions.
  • Simulate the production analysis code, not a reimplementation.
  • Report Monte Carlo error with every simulated rate.
  • Size non-standard designs by simulation, and keep the simulation in the repository so the next test can reuse it.
  • Re-run the checks when the metric definition, traffic mix or platform changes.

Self-test

You can run 500 or 5,000 simulations. When does the extra cost pay?

Show answerHide answer

When the answer is close to a threshold: a false positive rate of 6% with ±1 point of simulation error cannot be distinguished from 5%. For a rate of 17%, 500 is plenty.

Nuances and contested ground

  • Cap, transform or leave the tail alone? Capping raises power but changes the metric; log transforms change what a "lift" means; leaving it alone is honest but slow. Teams disagree, and the answer depends on whether big spenders' behaviour is the point.
  • Simulate from a model or resample? Resampling keeps real quirks but cannot extrapolate to new conditions; models can, but carry their assumptions.
  • How realistic must the effect be? A uniform multiplicative lift is convenient and often wrong. Effects concentrated in a few users make power much lower.
  • Does a passing A/A simulation prove a test is valid? It proves it for the simulated conditions. Real platforms have bucketing, logging and timing issues that only real A/A tests catch.

Self-test

Make the case against capping revenue in experiments.

Show answerHide answer

Big spenders can be where the effect is, and a cap hides changes in their behaviour. It also makes the reported lift a lift in a capped metric, which finance cannot use directly. Better variance reduction, such as CUPED, may give the power without changing the metric.

Interview ladder

Rung 1 · Recall the idea

What is an A/A test, and why would you simulate many of them?

Follow-ups, weak and strong answersHide
  • What should the p-values look like?
  • How many simulations?

Weak

"An A/A test checks that two identical groups look the same."

Strong

An A/A test compares two arms with no difference, so any significant result is a false positive. Simulating many, by resampling real users, measures the method's actual false positive rate, which should be 5% with uniform p-values. A single A/A test cannot measure a rate. A thousand or more simulations give about ±0.7 points of precision.

"The formula, because it is exact."

Strong
