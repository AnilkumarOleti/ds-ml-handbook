Price and demand elasticity

How much demand falls when price rises decides every fee, commission and price change. Historical prices follow demand, so the raw relationship misleads, sometimes with the wrong sign. Tests or natural experiments give the elasticity; the decision needs every margin that rides on each order.

Why it exists

LeadIC

Try it first

Spend two or three minutes on this before reading anything else.

A marketplace charges buyers a ₹40 delivery fee on 100,000 orders a day and earns ₹80 commission per order. Finance proposes raising the fee to ₹50.

An analyst regresses log orders on log fee across 300 city-weeks and finds an elasticity of +0.77: "higher fees go with more orders". A two-week switchback test that alternated ₹40 and ₹50 by city and day finds −0.4.

Which elasticity do you believe? Should the fee go up?

Show answerHide answer

Believe the test. Across cities, fees were set higher where demand was strong, so the regression mixes "high demand" with "high fee" and even gets the sign wrong. In the simulated data behind it, adding a demand index recovers −0.39. The switchback randomised the fee, so demand cannot confound it.

Then do the arithmetic for the whole business. At −0.4, a 25% fee rise cuts orders by 1 − 1.25−0.4 = 8.5%, to 91,460 a day.

  • Fee revenue: ₹40 lakh → ₹45.7 lakh a day. Finance's view: +₹5.7 lakh.
  • Commission lost: 8,540 orders × ₹80 = ₹6.8 lakh.
  • Net: −₹1.1 lakh a day.

The fee rise loses money once lost commission is counted. It would break even only if demand were less sensitive than −0.36. 1

Two analogies

A cinema's ticket price. On Saturdays, tickets cost more and the hall is full; on Tuesdays, cheap and empty. Plot price against attendance and higher prices seem to fill seats. The cinema set prices high on Saturdays because people come anyway.

A café's coffee and pastries. Raising the coffee price earns more per cup and loses some customers, and each lost customer also takes a pastry sale with them.

Price and demand move together for reasons other than the price itself, and a price change affects everything a lost customer would have bought, so both the measurement and the decision must look past the price line alone.

Where each analogy breaks

  • A cinema has one product. Marketplaces have thousands of sellers' prices and the platform's own fees, and buyers compare across them: cross-price effects matter.
  • The café sees its customers. Online, a price rise may shift buyers to a competitor, delay the purchase, or change the basket, each with a different long-run cost.
  • Both are short-run. Elasticities grow over time as buyers find alternatives; a two-week test measures the short run.

Self-test

Why?Why can a regression of quantity on price across stores give a positive elasticity even when every store's customers dislike higher prices?

Show answerHide answer

Stores with high demand set higher prices. Across stores, the price variation mostly reflects demand, so the regression picks up demand's effect on quantity. It is confounding, also called endogeneity.

Concept

price elasticity of demand ε = %Δ quantity / %Δ price ≈ Δ ln Q / Δ ln P |ε| < 1 inelastic: revenue from the price rises with price |ε| > 1 elastic: revenue from the price falls with price constant-elasticity form Q = Q₀ (P / P₀)^ε (a local approximation) cross-price elasticity %Δ quantity of B / %Δ price of A (+ substitutes, − complements) estimating it randomised price test (user, city, time; switchbacks) best natural experiments: tax or cost changes, rule changes (IV) regression with controls or DML confounded conjoint or survey stated, not revealed deciding total contribution = (price margin + other margin per unit) × Q(P) profit-maximising markup (P − MC) / P = −1 / ε (single product)
  • Endogeneity is the central problem: prices are set by people who see demand. Only variation unrelated to demand identifies the elasticity: randomisation, or an instrument such as a cost shock that moves price but not demand. 2
  • Observational methods such as double ML with rich controls can help where tests are impossible, but they still assume the confounders are measured.
  • Marketplaces have several prices: sellers' item prices, buyer fees, seller fees. A change in one affects volume, and every margin earned on that volume.
  • Short and long run differ: buyers learn, compare and switch. Long holdouts, or tests that persist, reveal the long-run response.

Self-test

Why?Demand is inelastic at −0.4. Does that mean the platform should raise the fee?

Show answerHide answer

Not on its own. Fee revenue rises, but each lost order also loses commission and possibly future purchases. Here the net effect is negative. Decide on total contribution, short and long run.

Why is a switchback by city and day a good design for a delivery fee test?

Show answerHide answer

Fees affect couriers and delivery times shared by everyone in a city, so user-level randomisation would interfere across arms. Alternating whole city-days avoids that, at the cost of fewer independent units; see interference.

How it works

Mechanics, faded

Worked: from elasticity to orders

A 25% fee rise with ε = −0.4: Q₁ ÷ Q₀ = 1.25−0.4 = 0.9146, so orders fall 8.5%. In logs: ln 0.9146 ÷ ln 1.25 = −0.089 ÷ 0.223 = −0.40. For small changes, "1% higher price, 0.4% fewer orders" is close enough; for 25%, use the power form.

Half-worked: the full business case

Net daily change = (new fee + commission) × new orders − (old fee + commission) × old orders. With ₹80 commission: at ε = −0.4, (50 + 80) × 91,461 − (40 + 80) × 100,000 = −₹1.1 lakh. Work out ε = −0.8, and the break-even elasticity.

Show answerHide answer

At −0.8: orders 83,651, fee revenue ₹41.8 lakh, commission lost ₹13.1 lakh, net −₹11.3 lakh a day. Break-even: 1.25ε = 120 ÷ 130, so ε = ln(0.923) ÷ ln(1.25) = −0.36. The fee rise pays only if demand is less sensitive than that. The same arithmetic shows lowering the fee to ₹30 at −0.4 would gain about ₹3.4 lakh a day, a test worth running.

Bare: reading the observational regression

Across 300 city-weeks the slope of log orders on log fee is +0.77. Adding a city demand index changes it to −0.39. A colleague asks why not just use the controlled regression and skip the test.

Show answerHide answer

Because the result depends on the demand index capturing everything that drove fee setting. If fees also responded to courier supply, competitor promotions or weather, those confound too, and nothing in the data says which were missed. The controlled estimate is a good prior and a check; the randomised test is what a pricing decision worth crores should rest on.

Predict, then play

Predict whether raising the fee could ever pay with ₹80 commission at ε = −0.2.

Show answerHide answer

Yes: −0.2 is less sensitive than the break-even −0.36. Orders fall 4.4%, fee revenue rises to ₹47.8 lakh, commission lost is ₹3.5 lakh, net about +₹4.3 lakh a day. The decision turns on where the elasticity sits relative to break-even, which is why its interval matters more than its point estimate.

Self-test

Why?Why cluster standard errors by city in the switchback analysis?

Show answerHide answer

Days within a city share unobserved conditions, so they are not independent. Treating every city-day as independent overstates precision. Cluster by city, or by the randomisation blocks.

A seller-side price test shows elasticity −2.5 for one seller's item. Why is the category's elasticity much smaller?

Show answerHide answer

One seller's buyers can switch to other sellers' identical items, so the seller faces very elastic demand. The category as a whole has fewer substitutes. Elasticity depends on the level at which the price changes.

Self-test

A test cut the fee from ₹40 to ₹0 for some users and orders rose 30%. Can you use that to predict ₹30?

Show answerHide answer

Not linearly. 'Free' has a special pull, so the jump from ₹1 to ₹0 is often larger than any other ₹1 step. Test ₹30 directly, or several points, before extrapolating.

Self-test

A government raised a tax that shifted delivery fees in one state only. What can you do with it?

Show answerHide answer

Use it as a natural experiment: compare that state's orders before and after with similar states, by difference-in-differences, treating the tax change as an instrument for the fee.

Applications in pairs

Pair 1

A buyer delivery fee.

A seller listing fee.

Pair 2

A premium shipping option priced above standard.

A subscription for free delivery.

What does each pair share?Hide

Pair 1 are platform fees with knock-on margins. Each lost order or listing also loses commission and, for sellers, supply that buyers see. Decide on total contribution across both sides.

Pair 2 involve cross-price effects. Pricing the premium option or the subscription shifts buyers between options; the question is the mix and total margin, not one option's demand curve.

A well-known misuse

Fitting a demand curve to historical prices and quantities and reading off the elasticity. The prices were chosen by people reacting to demand, so the curve is a mix of supply and demand, and can slope upwards. Economists have called this the identification problem for a century. 2

Self-test

An analyst plots monthly average price against units for a category over three years and finds a positive slope. What would you tell them?

Show answerHide answer

Prices rose in high-demand months and seasons, so the plot traces demand shifts, not a demand curve. To estimate elasticity, use variation in price unrelated to demand: a test, or a cost or tax shock.

In practice

  • Test prices whenever you can, with designs that respect interference and fairness.
  • Test more than one price point to see the shape, especially near thresholds such as free.
  • Decide on total contribution, including other margins lost with each order, and on the break-even elasticity.
  • Report the interval and where it sits against break-even.
  • Watch the long run with a holdout at the old price for a while.
  • Use observational elasticities as priors, with stated confounders, not as the basis for large decisions.

Self-test

The elasticity interval is −0.2 to −0.6 and break-even is −0.36. What do you recommend?

Show answerHide answer

The data cannot tell yet whether the rise pays. Run the test longer or with more cities to narrow the interval, or test a smaller change. Do not roll out on the point estimate.

Nuances and contested ground

  • Is price testing fair? Showing different prices to different buyers can feel unfair and may be regulated. Switchbacks by time and place, or testing on new users, reduce the concern; some companies avoid it entirely.
  • Short-run tests, long-run decisions. A two-week test may understate how buyers adapt. Some teams keep long holdouts; others accept the risk.
  • Fees or commission? Marketplaces debate who should pay, buyers or sellers. Elasticities on each side, and how each side's volume affects the other, decide it.
  • Personalised pricing. Elasticities by segment invite different prices per buyer, which is profitable and contentious.

Self-test

Make the case against personalised delivery fees, even if elasticities differ by segment.

Show answerHide answer

Buyers who learn they pay more lose trust; regulators may object; and the extra margin may be small against the risk. Uniform, simple fees with targeted promotions can capture most of the value.

Rung 1 · Recall the idea

What is price elasticity, and how would you estimate it?

Follow-ups, weak and strong answersHide
  • Why not regress quantity on price?
  • What is break-even elasticity?

Weak

"Regress sales on price."

Strong

The percentage change in quantity for a 1% change in price, often estimated as the slope in logs. Historical prices respond to demand, so a raw regression is confounded and can even be positive. Use randomised price tests, switchbacks for shared supply, or natural experiments such as cost or tax shocks as instruments; observational methods with controls only as priors.

Rung 3 · What ifIC

An observational regression gives +0.77, a switchback gives −0.4. Explain.

Follow-ups, weak and strong answersHide

  • Why cluster in the switchback?

Weak

"The regression has too little data."

Strong

Fees were set higher where demand was strong, so across city-weeks the regression measures demand's effect on orders: endogeneity. The switchback randomised the fee. An instrument would be something that moved fees without moving demand, such as a courier cost change or a tax. Cluster by city because days within a city share conditions.

Rung 4 · DesignLeadIC

Design a pricing study for a marketplace's buyer fees.

Follow-ups, weak and strong answersHide
  • How do you handle interference and fairness?
  • What is the decision rule?

Weak

"A/B test two fees on users."

Strong

Switchbacks or geo tests across several fee levels including a lower one, randomised by city and time, long enough for weekly cycles; outcomes: orders, GMV, fee revenue, commission, delivery times, repeat purchase; clustered intervals; decision on total contribution with break-even elasticity; a small long-run holdout at the old fee; legal and fairness review before launch.
