Anomaly detection and alerting thresholds

An alert is only useful if someone still reads it. Comparing each day with a forecast rather than a flat average, using robust spreads and persistence rules, and setting thresholds from a false alert budget across all series is what lets a monitor catch real drops without crying wolf.

Act 1

Why it exists

1
LeadICMLE

Try it first

Spend two or three minutes on this before reading anything else.

You monitor daily orders for 40 categories in 5 regions: 200 series. The current rule alerts when a day is more than 3 standard deviations from the series' 28-day average. The on-call analyst gets about 80 alerts a month and has started ignoring them. Last month a checkout bug cut one category's orders by 10% for four days and nobody noticed.

Daily orders in that category swing ±15% over the week; a forecast that knows the day of week is off by about 4% on a typical day.

Why so many false alerts? Why was a 10% drop missed? What would you change?

Show answerHide answer

Too many alerts. Even with perfectly normal noise, 200 series × 30 days × 0.27% gives about 16 false alerts a month. Real day-to-day changes have heavier tails: with residuals shaped like a t-distribution, the same rule gives about 80. And the 28-day average ignores the weekly cycle, so every Sunday looks strange.

The miss. Against raw daily variation of 15%, a 10% drop is only 0.7 standard deviations, invisible. Against a forecast that knows the day of week, it is 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5 standard deviations: visible, though below a 3σ single-day threshold most days.

The fix. Compare each day with a forecast (day of week, holidays, trend), not a flat average. Measure the residuals' spread robustly. Use a persistence rule, such as two consecutive days beyond 2σ in the same direction: about 6 false alerts a month under normal noise, and a 63% chance of catching a 2.5σ drop within three days, close to the single-day rule's 67%. Then set the threshold from the false alert budget the team can actually handle, and route alerts by size and business impact.

Two analogies

A car alarm. One set off by every passing lorry is soon ignored by the whole street, including on the night the car is actually stolen. A good alarm is quiet often enough that people still look up when it sounds.

A fever thermometer. 37.8°C means something different at 6 a.m. than after a run. A doctor reads it against what is normal for that person, at that time, in that state.

IdeaCar alarmThermometerMetric alerts
The baselineA quiet streetNormal for this person at this timeA forecast: day of week, holidays, trend
The thresholdHow hard a knock sets it offHow far above normalHow many standard deviations of the residual
False alarmsLorriesExercise, hot dayNormal noise, heavy tails, unmodelled seasonality

What do the two share? One sentence.

Compare with mineHide

An alarm is judged against the right baseline and by the balance between missing real problems and crying wolf, because an alarm that is ignored catches nothing.

Where each analogy breaks

  • A car alarm watches one car. A monitoring system watches hundreds of series, so even rare false alarms add up: it is a multiple testing problem.
  • A fever is always bad. A metric spike can be good news, a campaign working. Alerts need a direction and an owner who knows the calendar.
  • Both alarms are physical. Metric alerts also fire on broken pipelines, where the data is wrong rather than the business. Data-quality checks should run first.

Self-test

Why?Why does adding more series to the monitor increase false alerts even if each series' rule is unchanged?

Show answerHide answer

Each series has its own small false alert rate, and they add up. 200 series at 0.27% a day give about half a false alert every day. It is the same arithmetic as multiple testing.

Concept

anomaly score = (observed − expected) / typical error expected a forecast: day of week, holidays, trend, events (seasonal naive, ETS, Prophet, ARIMA, regression) typical error robust spread of past residuals e.g. 1.4826 × median absolute deviation alert rules single point |score| > k persistence m of the last n days beyond k′ on the same side cumulative CUSUM or EWMA: catches small sustained shifts budget false alerts per month ≈ series × days × P(|score| > k) choose k from the budget, not from habit precision true alerts ÷ all alerts; falls as incidents get rarer
  • The forecast is the baseline. Most false alerts in practice come from a baseline that ignores weekly cycles, holidays and planned events. See forecasting, baselines and backtests.
  • Robust spread. A standard deviation computed from a window that contains last month's outage is inflated, and hides the next one. The median absolute deviation ignores a few extreme days.
  • Persistence and cumulative rules trade a day or two of delay for far fewer false alarms, and catch sustained small shifts that single-day rules miss. 1
  • It is a base-rate problem. If real incidents are rare, most alerts are false even with a good detector; see Bayes' rule and base rates.
  • Size matters more than surprise. A 50% drop in a tiny category is statistically louder than a 2% drop in the largest. Rank alerts by estimated impact in orders or revenue.

Self-test

Why?Why is a 3σ threshold not a fixed 0.27% false alarm rate in practice?

Show answerHide answer

That number assumes normal residuals with a correctly estimated spread. Real residuals have heavier tails, and spreads estimated from short windows are noisy, so the true rate is usually several times higher.

A metric drifts down 1% a day for two weeks. Which rule catches it?

Show answerHide answer

A cumulative rule such as CUSUM or EWMA, or a trend check. Each day is unremarkable on its own; the sum is not.

Act 2

How it works

4
LeadICMLE

Mechanics, faded

Worked: the false alert budget

  • Normal residuals, 3σ, 200 series, 30 days: 6,000 × 0.0027 = 16 false alerts a month.
  • Residuals shaped like a t-distribution with 4 degrees of freedom, scaled to the same standard deviation: P(|z| > 3) = 1.3%, so 79 a month, matching what the analyst sees.
  • For one false alert a month under normal residuals, the per-test rate must be 1 ÷ 6,000, which needs a threshold of 3.8σ. That is too blunt to catch a 2.5σ drop.

So threshold-tuning alone cannot win. The spread of the baseline must shrink (a better forecast), and the rule must use more than one day.

Half-worked: two days instead of one

Rule B alerts when two consecutive days are beyond 2σ in the same direction. Under normal residuals, the false alert chance per series-day is 2 × (0.0228)² = 0.00104. A 2.5σ drop puts each day beyond −2σ with probability Φ(0.5) = 0.69. Find B's false alerts per month, its chance of catching the drop within three days, and the precision of each rule if there are two real incidents a month.

Show answerHide answer

B: 6,000 × 0.00104 = 6.2 false alerts a month. Detection within three days: 2 × 0.69² − 0.69³ = 0.63. The single-day 3σ rule: within three days 1 − (1 − 0.31)³ = 0.67, with 16 false alerts. With two incidents a month, rule A gives 1.3 true alerts among 17.5, 8% precision; rule B gives 1.25 among 7.5, 17%. Better, and still mostly false: base rates bite. Routing by impact and pairing alerts with data-quality checks are what make the queue workable.

Bare: what goes in the alert

The on-call analyst gets the alert "electronics, south: −2.6σ". What should the message contain so that the analyst can decide in two minutes whether to act?

Show answerHide answer

The observed and expected values and their gap in orders and rupees; how unusual it is and how long it has lasted; whether the region or the category as a whole moved too; whether pipelines and data-quality checks passed; known events that day; a link to the series chart with the forecast band; and the owner. Impact in rupees decides urgency; context decides whether it is real.

Predict, then play

Predict how many false alerts a month the 3σ single-day rule gives on 200 series if residuals have heavy tails, and how far the threshold must rise to bring it back to about 16.

Show answerHide answer

About 80. With these heavy tails, getting back to 16 needs a threshold of about 4.7σ, which makes moderate drops nearly undetectable. Heavy tails are an argument for persistence rules and robust spreads, not just higher thresholds.

import numpy as np

resid = actual - forecast                       # forecast knows weekday, holidays, events
scale = 1.4826 * np.median(np.abs(resid[-56:] - np.median(resid[-56:])))   # robust spread
z = resid / scale
same_side = np.sign(z[-1]) == np.sign(z[-2])
alert = same_side and min(abs(z[-1]), abs(z[-2])) > 2       # two days beyond 2 sigma
impact = resid[-2:].sum()                       # orders lost or gained; rank alerts by this

Self-test

Why?Why compute the spread over the last 56 days with the median absolute deviation rather than the standard deviation?

Show answerHide answer

A single outage in the window inflates the standard deviation, widening the band and hiding the next incident. The MAD barely moves for a few extreme days. Eight weeks gives enough points and covers weekday patterns.

Why might you set a different threshold for the ten largest categories than for the rest?

Show answerHide answer

Their incidents cost more, so missing one is worse; and their series are less noisy relative to their size. A lower threshold there buys detection where it matters, while a higher one elsewhere keeps the total false alert budget.

Checked data freshness and completeness first and suppressed anomaly alerts for late data; and grouped simultaneous alerts into one, pointing at the shared cause. See data quality, contracts and observability.

Applications in pairs

Pair 1

Daily GMV by category. Weekly cycle, festival peaks.

Hourly payment failures. Daily cycle, sudden spikes.

Pair 2

Seller sign-ups slowly declining for a month.

A fraud rate creeping up.

What does each pair share?Hide

Pair 1 needs seasonal baselines. Each has a strong cycle, so a flat average alerts on every peak and trough. Forecast residuals with robust thresholds, and for payment failures, a short persistence rule because minutes matter.

Pair 2 are slow drifts. No single day is alarming. Cumulative rules (CUSUM, EWMA) or a weekly trend check against a forecast catch them; single-day thresholds never will.

A well-known misuse

Tuning thresholds by complaint. The team lowers thresholds after a missed incident and raises them after a noisy week, so the system swings between deafening and silent. The fix is to agree a false alert budget, measure detection on past incidents, and change the baseline or the rule rather than the number.

Self-test

After a missed incident, a VP asks for thresholds to be lowered to 2σ everywhere. What do you show them?

Show answerHide answer

The arithmetic: 200 series at 2σ give about 270 false alerts a month under ideal conditions, more in practice, and the team will stop reading them. Then show what would have caught the missed incident with a few false alerts: a better baseline and a persistence rule.

In practice

  • Start from a false alert budget the on-call team agrees to, such as five a week, and work back to thresholds.
  • Use a forecast with events as the baseline; maintain the event calendar.
  • Use robust spreads on a log scale for volume metrics.
  • Combine rules: single-day for large shocks, persistence for moderate ones, cumulative for drifts.
  • Run data-quality checks first and suppress anomaly alerts on late or incomplete data.
  • Rank and route by impact, with context in the message.
  • Review monthly: false alerts, missed incidents, time to detect.

Self-test

How would you measure whether a new alerting system is better than the old one?

Show answerHide answer

Replay the past year: for each, count false alerts and how many known incidents were caught and how fast. Compare at the same false alert budget. Then watch the live precision and missed incidents for a quarter.

Nuances and contested ground

  • Statistical or ML detectors? Forecast-residual methods are transparent and easy to tune; ML detectors find odd combinations but are hard to explain and to trust. Most teams start simple and add ML for specific blind spots.
  • Who owns an alert? Analytics, engineering or the business owner. Unowned alerts are ignored. The usual answer is to route by series to a named owner.
  • Alert on everything or on what matters? Monitoring every cut catches more, at the cost of noise. Monitoring the few metrics tied to decisions keeps attention but misses local problems.
  • Should good news alert? A spike can be a campaign or a bot attack. Many teams alert both ways and route positive spikes at lower priority.

Self-test

Make the case for monitoring fewer series.

Show answerHide answer

Each series adds false alerts, and attention is finite. A hierarchy that watches totals closely and drills down on demand catches most incidents that matter with far less noise.
